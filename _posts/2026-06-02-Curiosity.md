### The Case Against the Curriculum

My favourite philosopher, Friedrich Wilhelm Nietzsche, began his academic career as a philologist. For those unfamiliar with the term, philology is essentially the rigorous study of language in historical sources—it is the deep dive into the history, development, and underlying architecture of words and texts. Nietzsche loved language. He possessed a mind that could extract profound meaning from the roots of words. Yet, for millions of other people, the academic study of literacy and grammar is enough to bore them to tears.

When it comes to literacy and numeracy, human brains are not identical hard drives waiting to be uploaded with the same software. Some people inherently love dissecting sentences; others naturally see the world in numbers. If you happen to be someone who dislikes a particular subject, why should you be forced to grind through it? Especially today, when the technological tools of the modern world can instantly perform the heavy lifting of basic numeracy and literacy for us.

---

### The Arithmetic Absurdity

I am absolutely rubbish at basic arithmetic. Adding, subtracting, multiplying, dividing in my head—I cannot stand it. It bores me, and my [brain]({{ "/case-studies/who-we-are/brain/" | relative_url }}) resists it.

Yet, paradoxically, I am extremely good at other mathematics subjects such as algebra, trigonometry, and calculus. I find the deeper concepts of mathematics endlessly fascinating. But to get to the mathematics that actually describes how the universe works, I first had to survive years of tedious early schooling where I constantly wondered: "What is the point of memorising times tables when I have a calculator?"

Today, that argument is even stronger. I have tools like the Python programming language at my fingertips, which can handle massive datasets and complex calculations in milliseconds. Why did I spend hundreds of hours of my brief childhood doing the mental equivalent of digging a ditch with a spoon when a backhoe was sitting right next to me?

I firmly believe that understanding the concepts of calculus—how things change, grow, and interact over time—is fundamentally more important to understanding reality than the rote memorization of arithmetic. Don't misunderstand me, I'm not suggesting kids learn calculus; rather, I'm claiming [traditional]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#tradition) schooling prioritizes the mechanical over the conceptual. 

---

### The Illusion of the "Higher" Class

This rigid, one-size-fits-all approach to learning leads to one of the most tragic [lock-ins]({{ "/case-studies/locked-in/" | relative_url }}) in our society: the categorization of human worth based on a narrow curriculum.

When I was in school, I was lucky enough to be placed in a "higher" class. But many of my peers were not so fortunate. They were filtered into "lower" tiers and implicitly told they were less intelligent. The reality? Many of them were incredibly streetwise, highly practical, and socially brilliant. They could have thrived if the school had valued vocational intelligence—if, for example, there had been classes in bicycle maintenance, carpentry, or practical engineering.

Instead, their [natural]({{ "/guide/human-history/natural-vs-normal/" | relative_url }}) talents were ignored because they didn't fit the curriculum. We force everyone through the exact same funnel, demanding they perform basic arithmetic that a £5 solar calculator can do, and we judge their life's potential on the outcome.

### The Sacred Cows of the Syllabus

This forced compliance extends beyond maths. We are forced to regurgitate the words of William Shakespeare, treating his texts as if they are [sacred]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#sacred), infallible documents containing the ultimate [meaning of life]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#meaning).

Don't get me wrong: subjectively, Shakespeare's works are wonderful. He possessed an unparalleled ability to conjure up profound combinations of words that express the human condition in a poignant, articulate manner. But we must be clear: nothing Shakespeare ever wrote is an [evidence-based]({{ "/guide/first-principles/evidence/" | relative_url }}) fact. It is art. It is [subjective]({{ "/guide/first-principles/intersubjective/" | relative_url }}) resonance.

His work, while culturally significant, can never equal the contributions of Newton, Darwin, or Einstein. Those individuals gave us [testable]({{ "/guide/first-principles/testability/" | relative_url }}), [objective]({{ "/guide/first-principles/intersubjective/" | relative_url }}) [facts]({{ "/guide/first-principles/correct-answers/" | relative_url }}) about the nature of reality. Their evidence-based ideas, when applied correctly, are what actually allow us to build technologies, cure diseases, and tangibly improve the human condition for all.

As I explored in the Case Study on [Aristotle]({{ "/case-studies/philosophy/aristotle/" | relative_url }}), we should never make idols out of any of these historical figures—it is their ideas, and the evidence supporting them, that matter. So why do we mandate the study of subjective 16th-century plays with the same rigidity as the laws of physics? Why shouldn't diving into the arts, or specific branches of science, or hands-on trades, be a matter of personal choice?

---

### Trusting the Evolved Brain

We have to fundamentally change the system. I believe we need to abolish the rigid "curriculum" entirely. It should be replaced by curiosity-based learning.

Does this mean we abandon schools? Absolutely not. Schools serve a vital social and educational purpose. But what we learn when we walk through those doors should be driven by [autonomy]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#autonomy). We should have a vast array of technologically assisted options. If a student is not (yet) ready or interested in a topic, they should have the option to "pull out" and focus on what currently drives them.

Perhaps their curiosity will circle back to that missed topic later in life when they actually need it. But for now, we are crushing the [innate]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#innate), [evolved]({{ "/guide/human-history/evolution/" | relative_url }}) human drive to learn by replacing it with industrial-era compliance. [Children]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#role-of-children) are naturally, relentlessly curious, and learning could (and should) be enjoyable. Instead, we bore children to death. It is time we stop forcing them to be compliant rule-followers unhealthly sitting at desks for extended periods, and start giving them the autonomy to construct their own destiny based on what they genuinely want to learn and who they want to become.
