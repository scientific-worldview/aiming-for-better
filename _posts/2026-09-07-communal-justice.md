### The Myth of Top-Down Justice: Why We Must Return Communal Arbitration

When a crime occurs in modern society, the machinery of the state kicks into gear. Absurd wigs flap about their legal bonces, tabloid headlines scream, and an adversarial system demands retribution. We call this "justice." But when viewed through an empirical, evolutionary lens, our modern criminal justice system is revealed to be a profound [evolutionary mismatch]({{ "/case-studies/mismatches/" | relative_url }})—a deeply flawed, top-down bureaucracy that outsources human arbitration to absolute strangers.

If we genuinely want to reduce harm, lower recidivism, and build pro-social adults, we have to ask a radical question: _Should justice be returned to the community?_{: .mark}

### Behind Closed Doors: The Blind Spot of Modernity

To understand why modern justice fails, we must first look at where modern crime often begins: in the [architecture of isolation]({{ "/case-studies/health/fortress-of-privacy/" | relative_url }}).

Because of the relatively recent invention of the isolated nuclear family, we have lost the horizontal visibility of the clan. We frequently have no idea what is happening behind closed doors. We don't see when a developing human being is subjected to chronic stress, physical coercion, or psychological trauma. In our ancestral environment, the community (a tribe of roughly 150 people) would have witnessed these dysfunctions immediately and intervened. Today, the abuse is hidden until the child grows up, their [biology]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#biology) completely rewired for hyper-vigilance and impulsivity, and they finally clash with the law.

When this traumatized individual commits an offence, the modern system asks: *How much should they suffer?* A biologically informed, communal system would ask: *What happened to them, and how do we fix it?*

### The Spectacle of Retribution: The Media and the Mob

We cannot talk about modern justice without confronting the massive, distorting influence of the media. The media does not exist to facilitate fairness; it exists to harvest attention by weaponising our hardwired tribal outrage. 

When an offence occurs, the tabloid press and social media algorithms instantly construct a moral panic. They strip away all context, biological reality, and nuance, reducing complex human tragedies into a simplistic binary of "evil monsters" and "flawless victims." This media spectacle directly corrupts the judicial process.

Judges—who are often insulated, upper-class individuals entirely disconnected from the material realities of the defendants they sentence—are not immune to this pressure. Instead of acting as neutral arbiters of community restitution, they frequently become performers, handing down draconian, performative sentences to satisfy a manufactured, baying mob. Justice becomes a spectator sport designed to sell ads and reinforce the moral superiority of the public, rather than a mechanism for actual sociological repair.

### The Priesthood of Strangers

Instead of a community that knows the offender, we rely on a [secular priesthood]({{ "/guide/obstacles/secular-priesthood/" | relative_url }}) of strangers. 

Before sentencing, courts rely on pre-sentence reports written by probation officers, forensic psychologists, and social workers. These individuals are complete strangers to the offender. They sit in sterile offices and write purportedly "accurate" reports using confident, professional-sounding language. 

But stripped of its authority, much of this is simply humanities-based rhetoric and sophistry. It is pseudoscience masquerading as empirical fact. These professionals frequently project their own biases onto the offender. Many enter the criminal justice system because they were victims themselves, or knew victims, leading to a highly adversarial, even hostile, approach to the people they are supposed to be objectively assessing. 

They do not genuinely care if an offence was a terrible mistake committed in the throes of addiction, an epigenetic trauma response, or a genuine display of anti-social character. They exist to process human beings through the bureaucracy of the Offender Treatment Industry.

### The Biological Baseline: Dunbar’s Justice

Is the idea of communal justice just a romantic vision? No. It is the evidence-based baseline of our species.

For over 95% of human history, humans lived in groups of roughly 150 individuals [Dunbar's number]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#dunbars-number). In these clans, justice was not administered by strangers. It was arbitrated by the community who actually knew the person. If someone offended, the community had the entire context of that person's life. They knew if the offender's parents were neglectful. They knew if the offender was grieving, addicted, or unwell. 

Because the community actually had a stake in the offender's future, the goal was almost always *restitution*, not retribution. A community has members who will fiercely advocate for the offender, and members who will advocate for the victim. Together, they arbitrate a fair outcome designed to put things right and reintegrate the individual.

### Emotion vs. Evidence: Reclaiming Impartiality

This brings us to the most difficult, yet necessary, aspect of a scientific approach to justice: the role of the victim. 

When someone is harmed, they and their families clamour for retribution. This is an entirely understandable, deeply human emotional response. We must validate their pain and support their recovery. However, it is utterly irrational—and highly dangerous—to allow those raw emotions to dictate the administration of justice.

When justice is driven by a victim's desire for vengeance (amplified by the media), it ceases to be justice. It becomes state-sanctioned retaliation. Impartiality requires us to set those emotions aside. A rational, evidence-based system does not allow victims to dictate sentences, parole, or the nature of restitution. That must be the job of an impartial, well-informed community.

### The Path Forward

We cannot dismantle massive nation-states or the top-down legal apparatus overnight. But we must urgently recognise their unsuitability for the human animal. 

We must begin to decentralize justice. We must build frameworks that return arbitration to local, Dunbar-sized community groups who can actually look at the evidence, understand the context of the trauma, and demand constructive restitution rather than relying on the absurdity of the prison cage. 

Only a community that truly knows its members can arbitrate fairly. Outsourcing justice to a media-driven mob and a priesthood of strangers will never heal the individual, and it will never protect the tribe.
