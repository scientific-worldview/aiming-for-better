An institution created to protect a ruler is a *double-edged sword*. While it serves as a necessary shield against external threats, it can also become an _internal prison_{: .warn}, [locking]({{ "/case-studies/locked-in/" | relative_url }}) the state into a cycle of [corruption]({{ "/case-studies/politics/bureaucratic-machine/corruption/" | relative_url }}) and instability. History is *littered with examples* of elite military units whose own survival and privilege became more important than the survival of the system they were sworn to defend. They are the ultimate example of the _Brute Force Lock-In_{: .neon}: an armed and organised _vested interest_{: .bold} that holds progress _hostage_{: .warn}. To understand this timeless pattern, we will examine two of the most infamous and consequential examples: the Roman Empire's Praetorian Guard and the Ottoman Empire's Janissaries. Though separated by a thousand years and a thousand miles, their stories are a chilling echo of one another—a _permanent warning_{: .bold} of what happens when the _guardians become the jailers_{: .bold}.

---

## The Praetorian Guard

![The Guard]({{ "/assets/images/casestudies/lock-ins/guard.webp" | relative_url }})

### Overview

In the early Roman Empire, the Praetorian Guard was the ultimate symbol of the Emperor's power. They were an elite military unit, stationed in Rome itself, tasked with a single, vital mission: to protect the life of the Emperor. They were the Secret Service of their day, a necessary shield against *assassination and chaos*.

But over time, this shield became a sword, and then a throne. The *protectors* became the _kingmakers_{: .bold}. The Praetorian Guard is the classic and brutal historical example of the _Lock-In Effect_{: .neon}. They were an [institution]({{ "/case-studies/locked-in/institutional-control/" | relative_url }}) created by a new political [narrative]({{ "/case-studies/narratives/" | relative_url }})—the rule of a single Emperor—whose own survival, wealth, and [power]({{ "/case-studies/politics/governance/power/" | relative_url }}) became so deeply tied to that narrative that _they would burn the world down rather than allow a return to the old system_{: .mark}.

This case study will explore how the Guard's _vested interest_{: .bold} held the Roman Empire hostage, demonstrating the simplest and most ruthless form of lock-in: _[brute force]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#brute-force)_{: .warn}.

---

### Step 1: Wipe the Slate Clean - The Narrative and the Need

Let's set aside the grand histories of Rome for a moment and look at the situation from a [clean slate]({{ "/guide/clean-slate/" | relative_url }}).

- _The Old Narrative:_{: .bullet} _The Roman Republic_{: .bold}. Power was (in theory) shared among the Senate and the people. The highest value was preventing any one man from becoming a king.


- _The New Narrative:_{: .bullet} _The Roman Empire_{: .bold}. [Framed]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#framing) as continuity (after a century of brutal civil wars) Augustus consolidated power in a single ruler, but under the story of **"First Citizen"** (Princeps). Rather than openly declaring a monarchy, he *maintained the appearance* of Republican institutions while ensuring that real authority rested with the Emperor—promising peace and stability (the _Pax Romana_{: .cool}).

- _The Practical Need:_{: .bullet} An all-powerful Emperor living in a city full of ambitious senators and rowdy mobs is a *prime target* for assassination. He needs an elite, loyal, and *utterly ruthless* bodyguard.

Thus, the Praetorian Guard was not initially a _corrupt institution_{: .warn}. It was a logical and necessary tool to protect and legitimise the new _Imperial System_{: .bold}.

---

### Step 2: Applying First Principles

#### Testability

Can we [test]({{ "/guide/first-principles/testability/" | relative_url }}) the effect of the Praetorian Guard? Yes. We can objectively track their actions through the historical records of writers like Tacitus and Suetonius. We can _count_{: .bold} the number of emperors they assassinated, the number they installed, and the _amount_{: .bold} of money they _extorted_{: .warn} from the state.

#### Objective vs. Intersubjective

- _Objective:_{: .bullet} The fact that the Guard _murdered_{: .warn} Emperor Caligula in 41 AD and immediately proclaimed his uncle Claudius as the new Emperor is *an objective, historical event*.

- _Intersubjective:_{: .bullet} Any claims the Guard supported, such that they were acting to *"restore order"* or *"ensure stability"*, is [intersubjective]({{ "/guide/first-principles/intersubjective/" | relative_url }}). The reality was often that they were _protecting their own power_{: .mark} (and securing a massive bonus from the new Emperor - the first of many!).

#### Only EVIDENCE Matters

We must reject the Guard's self-serving justifications and look at the [evidence]({{ "/guide/first-principles/evidence/" | relative_url }}) of their actions. 

_Did they consistently act to protect the stability of the Empire, or to advance their own interests?_{: .subtle}

---

### Step 3: Identify the Consequences - The Lock-In Mechanism in Action

Once the Imperial system was in place, the Praetorian Guard rapidly evolved from protectors to _parasites_{: .warn}. Their _vested interest_{: .bold} was not in a *good* Emperor or a *stable* Empire, but simply in the *existence* of an Emperor whose power they could manipulate.

This created a Lock-In Effect with *measurable consequences*:

- _The Assassination of Emperors:_{: .bullet} The Guard became the _ultimate arbiter_{: .bold} of who ruled. If an Emperor tried to discipline them (like Galba) or failed to pay them enough, they would simply murder him and install a more pliable candidate. They were a constant source of *instability* at the very heart of the Empire.

- _The Extortion of the State:_{: .bullet} New Emperors were forced to pay the Guard an enormous **"donative"** (a massive cash bonus) upon their accession. Claudius was the first to do this, and the practice became standard. The Guard was effectively _extorting_{: .warn} the Roman treasury, *draining resources* that could have been used for infrastructure or frontier defence.

- _The Prevention of Reform:_{: .bullet} The existence of this powerful, armed body in Rome made any thought of returning to the old Republic impossible. Even if the Senate and the people had desired a return to the old ways, they were _held hostage_{: .warn} by a military force of 9,000 elite soldiers whose *entire existence* **depended** on the _Imperial System_{: .bold}. They were the _ultimate veto power_{: .cool} on *political reform*.

- _The Auction of the Empire:_{: .bullet} The most infamous example of the lock-in came in 193 AD, the **"Year of the Five Emperors"**. After murdering Emperor Pertinax, the Praetorian Guard literally *auctioned off* the Roman Empire to the highest bidder. Two wealthy senators, Didius Julianus and Sulpicianus, bid against each other, offering ever-larger sums to the soldiers. Didius Julianus won, paying a staggering amoun for the throne. He lasted 66 days before he too was killed.

---

### Step 4: Reflect on the Vested Interest

_This is the Brute Force Lock-In in its purest form._{: .subtle}

- _The Narrative:_{: .bullet} "The *Imperium Romanum* must be preserved—whatever the cost"

- _The Institution:_{: .bullet} The Praetorian Guard.

- _The Vested Interest:_{: .bullet} The Guard's immense *pay*, privileged [status]({{ "/case-studies/society/sociology/status/" | relative_url }}), political *power*, and very *identity* were tied to this narrative.

- _The Defence Mechanism:_{: .bullet} When their *vested interest* was threatened (by a disciplined Emperor or a lack of payment), they used their _monopoly on force_{: .warn} within the city to _eliminate the threat_{: .warn} and install a more favorable regime.

They did not care if Tiberius was paranoid, Caligula was insane, or Nero was a tyrant. *The quality of the Emperor was irrelevant*. 

_All that mattered was that an Emperor existed to justify their existence and pay their salaries._{: .mark}

![The Guard]({{ "/assets/images/casestudies/lock-ins/praetorian.webp" | relative_url }})

---

### Conclusion: A Verdict on Power and Self-Preservation

The story of the Praetorian Guard is a timeless and _brutal lesson_{: .warn} in *how institutions function*. It teaches us that:

- _Institutions Prioritise Their Own Survival:_{: .bullet} An institution's primary goal is _not_{: .warn} necessarily to fulfill its stated mission, but to _ensure its own continued existence and power_{: .mark}.

- _Narratives Create Vested Interests:_{: .bullet} A powerful narrative will always create a class of people whose livelihood _depends on that narrative remaining "true"_{: .mark}.

- _Vested Interests are the Enemy of Progress:_{: .bullet} This class will become the most ferocious defenders of the [status quo]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#status-quo), often using the very power granted to them by the system to _prevent_{: .warn} any attempt *to reform or replace it*.

The Praetorian Guard is an *ancient example*, but the _mechanism is recurring_{: .bold}. When we look at modern institutions—from police unions _resisting reform_{: .warn}, to massive military budgets, to bureaucratic agencies that have *outlived their purpose*—we are seeing the same dynamic at play.

They may not use swords to auction off the state, but the lock-in effect of their vested interest can be just as powerful, holding [society]({{ "/case-studies/society/social-structures/" | relative_url }}) _hostage_{: .warn} and _preventing it from moving towards a more [rational]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#rational), evidence-based, and effective way of organizing itself._{: .mark}

_The first question we must always ask of any powerful institution is not "What is your mission?" but "What is your vested interest?"_{: .subtle}

## The Ottoman Janissaries

### Overview

At the height of its power, the **Ottoman Empire** possessed one of the most formidable and feared military forces on Earth: the _Janissaries_{: .bold}. Forged from a brutal system of *slave soldiery*, they were the elite infantry of the Sultan, a fanatically loyal and disciplined force that secured his throne and expanded his empire. For centuries, they were the razor's edge of Ottoman power.

![The Janissaries]({{ "/assets/images/casestudies/lock-ins/jans.webp" | relative_url }})

But like the Praetorian Guard in Rome, this *instrument* of [power]({{ "/case-studies/politics/governance/power/" | relative_url }}) eventually turned on its creator. The Janissaries evolved from the Sultan's loyal slaves into his masters, becoming a deeply entrenched, *reactionary force* whose own **vested interests** actively _prevented_{: .warn} the modernisation and survival of the very empire _they were created to protect_{: .mark}.

The story of the Janissaries is a powerful, parallel example of the _Brute Force Lock-In Effect_{: .neon}. It demonstrates, once again, the timeless and dangerous principle that an elite military body, once its own survival becomes its primary goal, will inevitably _hold the state hostage_{: .mark}.

---

### Step 1: Wipe the Slate Clean - The Narrative and the Need

Let's look at the origins of the Janissaries from a [clean slate]({{ "/guide/clean-slate/" | relative_url }}).

- _The Narrative:_{: .bullet} The Sultan, as the absolute ruler of a vast and often *rebellious* empire, requires a military force that is loyal only to him personally, not to regional lords, [tribes]({{ "/case-studies/society/social-structures/the-clan/" | relative_url }}), or their own families.

- _The Practical Need:_{: .bullet} How do you create such a force? Mercenaries are unreliable. Levies from powerful provinces can be disloyal.

- _The Radical Solution (The Devşirme System):_{: .bullet} The Ottomans devised a unique and brutal solution. They would levy a "tax" on Christian families in their Balkan territories, taking their young sons. These boys were forcibly converted to Islam, stripped of their family connections, and raised in a ferociously disciplined environment to become elite soldiers and administrators. Their only **"father"** was the Sultan.

This system, for its first two centuries, was terrifyingly effective. It produced a professional, celibate, and fanatically loyal military corps that was the envy of the world. It was a rational, if ruthless, solution to the problem of _securing centralized power_{: .bold}.

---

### Step 2: Applying First Principles

#### Testability

Yes, we can test the effects of the Janissaries. We can objectively track their military victories in the early centuries. We can also *document* their increasing involvement in politics, the *number* of Sultans they *deposed or murdered*, and their *resistance* to military reforms in the later centuries.

#### Objective vs. Intersubjective

- _Objective:_{: .bullet} The fact that the Janissaries *revolted and murdered* Sultan Osman II in 1622 after he attempted to create a new, rival army is an _objective, historical event_{: .bold}.

- _Intersubjective:_{: .bullet} The Janissaries' narrative was that they were protecting the **"ancient traditions"** of the Empire and their own honour. The reality was that they were _protecting their privileged position_{: .mark} from a **threat**.

#### Only EVIDENCE Matters

We must reject the Janissaries' self-serving narrative of [tradition]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#tradition) and look at the evidence. 

_Did their actions in the later period strengthen the Ottoman Empire or accelerate its decline?_{: .subtle}

---

### Step 3: Identify the Consequences - The Lock-In Mechanism in Action

As the centuries passed, the Janissaries' discipline eroded, and their political power grew. They became a classic example of an institution whose primary goal shifted from serving the state to _serving itself_{: .mark}.

- _The Shift from Military Elite to Entrenched Social Class:_{: .bullet} The Janissaries won the right to marry, enroll their own children in the corps, and engage in business. They ceased to be an elite slave army and became a powerful, hereditary social and economic class with deep roots in the capital, Istanbul.

- _The Kingmakers and Kingbreakers:_{: .bullet} Just like the Praetorians, they became the _ultimate arbiters_{: .bold} of power. They launched numerous revolts, often over pay or in response to perceived threats to their status. They deposed and murdered multiple Sultans, becoming a source of _chronic instability_{: .warn} at the heart of the government.

- _The Fierce Resistance to Modernisation:_{: .bullet} This was their most _damaging legacy_{: .warn}. As European military technology and tactics advanced in the 17th and 18th centuries, the Janissaries became an obsolete fighting force. Yet, they fiercely and violently resisted every attempt by reform-minded Sultans to modernise the army. Why? Because a new, modern army trained in European methods would have made the Janissaries irrelevant and destroyed their _political power_{: .bold} and privileges.

- _The "Auspicious Incident" (1826):_{: .bullet} The lock-in was so absolute that it could only be broken by _extreme violence_{: .warn}. Sultan Mahmud II, having secretly created a loyal, modern artillery corps, finally moved against the Janissaries. When they revolted, he had their barracks bombarded with artillery, killing thousands. The corps was brutally and violently abolished. The state had to literally _destroy its own creation_{: .mark} to escape the lock-in.

---

### Conclusion: A Verdict on the Danger of Entrenched Elites

The story of the Janissaries is a powerful echo of the Praetorian Guard, reinforcing a timeless lesson about power and _vested interests_{: .bold}.

- _Innovation Becomes Dogma:_{: .bullet} A system that was once a brilliant, innovative solution (a loyal slave army) became a rigid, traditionalist [dogma]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#dogma) that actively _prevented further innovation_{: .warn}.

- _Privilege Breeds Reaction:_{: .bullet} Once a group acquires special privileges, its primary political goal becomes the _defence of those privileges_{: .mark} against any change, even if that change is necessary for the [survival]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#survival) of the wider society.

- _A Violent Lock-In Requires a Violent End:_{: .bullet} When a powerful, armed group has a vested interest in the status quo, they will not be persuaded by _rational arguments_{: .bold}. The historical evidence suggests that such a lock-in can often only be broken by a _superior show of force_{: .mark}.

## Overall Conclusion

The stories of the Praetorians and the Janissaries, though unique in their details, deliver a single, unified verdict. Creating an elite institution with a monopoly on force and a deep vested interest in the central power structure is a _recurring and deadly mistake_{: .mark}.

_They are a permanent warning that the very institutions designed to protect a system can become the greatest obstacle to its ability to adapt and survive._{: .subtle}

### Reflect on Human History - The Great Mismatch

The recurring tragedy of the Praetorians and the Janissaries is not just a story about greed or military power. It is a story of a profound [mismatch]({{ "/case-studies/mismatches/" | relative_url }}) between our [evolved]({{ "/guide/human-history/evolution/" | relative_url }}) social instincts and the artificial structures of a large-scale empire.

- _The Mismatch of Loyalty and Scale:_{: .bullet} For over 99.5% of our [history]({{ "/guide/human-history/" | relative_url }}), our fiercest loyalties were directed towards our small, tangible kin-group. This loyalty was [reciprocal]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#reciprocity) and based on a lifetime of personal relationships. The state, however, requires a new kind of loyalty—an abstract, impersonal allegiance to an Emperor, a Sultan, or the **"Empire"** itself.

- _The Creation of an Artificial Tribe:_{: .bullet} To solve this, the state created an _artificial tribe_{: .cool}: an elite military unit, isolated from the general population, and indoctrinated to be loyal only to the ruler. The Praetorians were recruited as elite soldiers given special privileges in Rome. The Janissaries were even more extreme—slave boys ripped from their families and raised to know the Sultan as their only **"father"**.

- _The Inevitable Outcome:_{: .bullet} This [experiment]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#experiment) was _doomed to fail_{: .warn}. You cannot erase millions of years of evolutionary programming. The deep, powerful instinct for group loyalty did not disappear; it simply reattached itself to the most immediate, tangible group available: the corps itself. The Praetorians became loyal to the Praetorians. The Janissaries became loyal to the Janissaries. Their **"us"** was no longer the Empire; it was their brothers in arms in the barracks. The **"them"** became everyone else, including the very ruler they were supposed to protect.

_The lock-in effect, therefore, is the predictable and tragic outcome of this mismatch._{: .subtle}

When you create a powerful, artificial tribe with a _monopoly on force_{: .warn} and a deep _vested interest_{: .bold} in its own privilege, its ancient, evolved tribal loyalty _will inevitably override its new, artificial loyalty to the state_{: .mark}.
