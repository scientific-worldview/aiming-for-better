### Overview: Rights – Invented or Inherent?

The concept of "[rights]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#rights)" forms the moral and legal bedrock of *much of modern political thought*. Whether we speak of human rights, natural rights, civil rights, or constitutional rights, these claims *assert entitlements that individuals possess*, often against the [power]({{ "/case-studies/politics/governance/power/" | relative_url }}) of the state or other individuals. The prevailing [narrative]({{ "/case-studies/narratives/" | relative_url }}) is that certain rights—like life, [liberty]({{ "/case-studies/law/pragmatic/liberty/" | relative_url }}), and the pursuit of happiness—are inherent, universal, and inalienable, deriving from our very nature as humans or from a divine source. *To deny someone a right is often seen as a fundamental moral transgression*.

![Rights]({{ "/assets/images/casestudies/politics/rights.webp" | relative_url }})

This case study will argue that, from a [scientific worldview]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#scientific-worldview), "rights" are not inherent, divinely ordained, or objectively discoverable in nature. Instead, they are powerful and pragmatic [intersubjective]({{ "/guide/first-principles/intersubjective/" | relative_url }}) inventions: _sophisticated social constructs_{: .cool} that have emerged over time to *solve specific problems* of large-scale [cooperation]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#cooperation), protect individual and group [well-being]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#wellbeing), and *constrain the* [abuse of power]({{ "/case-studies/politics/bureaucratic-machine/corruption/" | relative_url }}). While the narrative of inherent rights is incredibly effective for mobilizing action and establishing legitimacy, the reality is that _rights are constantly contested and reinterpreted._{: .mark} They only exist to the extent that a [society]({{ "/case-studies/society/social-structures/" | relative_url }}) is willing and able to enforce them. We will examine their [evolutionary]({{ "/guide/human-history/" | relative_url }}) precursors, the historical circumstances that led to their invention, and the [lock-in]({{ "/case-studies/locked-in/" | relative_url }}) mechanisms that sustain them, as well as the "[mismatches]({{ "/case-studies/mismatches/" | relative_url }})" that arise *when abstract rights clash with biological imperatives* or the *realities of resource scarcity*.

### Background Information

The idea of rights has evolved significantly, from ancient privileges to universal declarations:

#### Ancient & Medieval Privileges

Early concepts of rights were often *tied to* [status]({{ "/case-studies/society/sociology/status/" | relative_url }}) *or specific groups* (e.g., the rights of a **Roman citizen**, the rights of a **nobleman**). They were more like *privileges or duties* within a **hierarchical system**, rather than universal entitlements. The idea of natural law, "discoverable by reason" and preceding human positive law, emerged in Greek philosophy and was later heavily developed by Christian theologians (e.g., [Thomas Aquinas]({{ "/case-studies/philosophy/christian-synthesis/" | relative_url }})), suggesting a higher moral order.

#### The Enlightenment (17th-18th Centuries)

This was the crucible for the modern concept of "natural rights".

- _John Locke:_{: .bullet} Argued that individuals possess *"inherent rights"* to life, liberty, and property, given by God or nature. Governments are formed via a [social contract]({{ "/case-studies/politics/governance/social-contract/" | relative_url }}) to protect these rights, and if they fail, the people have a right to revolution.

- _American & French Revolutions:_{: .bullet} The Lockean ideas were codified in foundational documents like the American Declaration of Independence (1776), asserting "unalienable Rights" including "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness", and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789), declaring "natural, unalienable and [sacred]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#sacred) rights of man". *These documents were revolutionary precisely because they asserted rights as universal rather than privilege-based.*

#### 19th & 20th Centuries

- _Emergence of Social and Economic Rights:_{: .bullet} Beyond "negative rights" (freedom from interference), movements for social justice (e.g., labour rights, women's suffrage) pushed for "positive rights" (entitlements to certain goods or services, like [education]({{ "/case-studies/society/social-structures/education/" | relative_url }}), healthcare, or a living wage).

- _Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR, 1948):_{: .bullet} Following the atrocities of World War II, the United Nations adopted the UDHR, which articulated a broad range of civil, political, economic, social, and cultural rights, intended to be *universally applicable to all people*. This marked a global effort to establish a _universal standard_{: .bold} for human dignity.

Throughout this history, the narrative of rights as "inherent" or "natural" has been a powerful force, lending moral weight and universality to what are, in practice, *human agreements and aspirations*.

### Step 1: Wipe the Slate Clean

Let’s set aside the *moral conviction* and the legal texts. We start with a [clean slate]({{ "/guide/clean-slate/" | relative_url }}), asking where such claims could logically originate.

#### The Problem

In any large, anonymous society, individuals face threats to their safety, [autonomy]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#autonomy), and access to resources from others and from powerful institutions (**especially the state**). This creates *vulnerability and potential for* [exploitation]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#exploitation).

#### The Stated Need

A mechanism to protect individuals from arbitrary harm and ensure a basic minimum standard of treatment and [opportunity]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#opportunity), allowing for greater social stability and individual [flourishing]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#human-flourishing).

#### The Prevailing Narrative (Inherent Rights)

Certain fundamental entitlements are intrinsic to being human, independent of government or [culture]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#culture). These "rights" exist prior to any law and are merely "recognized" or "declared" by legal systems, not created by them. *This narrative* [frames]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#framing) *rights as "objective moral facts"*.

This narrative implies that if we could just "look closely enough" at [human nature]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#human-nature), we would discover these "self-evident" truths.

---

### Step 2: Applying First Principles

#### Testability

The existence of inherent rights is not directly [testable]({{ "/guide/first-principles/testability/" | relative_url }}) in the scientific sense; they are not [empirical]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#empirical) phenomena. However, the effects of societies that embrace (or reject) rights are highly testable:

- _Social Outcomes:_{: .bullet} We can objectively compare societies that robustly protect certain rights (e.g., [free speech]({{ "/case-studies/politics/miscelleneous/free-speech/" | relative_url }}), property rights) against those that do not, measuring outcomes like *innovation, economic growth, social stability, and individual well-being*.

- _Psychological Impact:_{: .bullet} We can study the psychological effects of having (or lacking) guaranteed rights, such as levels of stress, trust in institutions, and willingness to participate in civic life.

- _Enforcement Mechanisms:_{: .bullet} We can analyse the efficacy of different legal and [political systems]({{ "/case-studies/politics/political-ideas/" | relative_url }}) in enforcing declared rights and identify the conditions under which rights are respected in practice.

#### Objective vs. Intersubjective

- _Objective:_{: .bullet} The observable fact that some societies permit free expression while others punish it is objective. The statistical [correlation]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#correlation) between property rights and economic development is objective. The physiological response of individuals to arbitrary detention is objective.

- _Intersubjective:_{: .bullet} The [belief]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#beliefs) that individuals possess a "right" to free expression, or that a particular action is a "violation" of a human right, is a powerful intersubjective narrative. These narratives are widely shared, deeply felt, and form the basis of collective moral and legal judgments, but *they are not independently verifiable* [natural]({{ "/guide/human-history/natural-vs-normal/" | relative_url }}) *phenomena*.

#### Only EVIDENCE Matters

We must move beyond the **philosophical assertion** of *"self-evident truths"* and look at *the functional role of rights in real human societies*.

- Are "rights" universal, or are they constantly being defined and redefined by *specific cultures* and *power structures*?

- What does our evolutionary history tell us about *"inherent entitlements"* or [reciprocal obligations]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#reciprocity)?

- What are the **practical consequences** of embracing a "rights-based" framework compared to alternative models?

### Step 3: Identify the Objective Consequences – The Pragmatic Power of Declared Entitlements

When we identify the objective consequences of "rights" in action, we see they function as _social technologies_{: .cool}, but they are only as real as the power structures that enforce them:

- _Constraining Power:_{: .bullet} The most profound consequence of declaring rights is their ability to serve as *a check on arbitrary power*, particularly that of the state. By asserting that individuals have entitlements that even the government cannot violate, rights provide a moral and legal basis for *resistance and accountability*. This directly *counters the potential for abuse* discussed in [Power: Its Nature and Control]({{ "/case-studies/politics/governance/power/" | relative_url }})

- _Enabling Cooperation:_{: .bullet} Rights (e.g., property rights, contract rights, due process) provide *a stable framework* that allows individuals and groups to cooperate and engage in complex transactions with *greater trust and predictability*. They reduce the "transaction costs" of social interaction and foster economic development.

- _Mobilizing Collective Action:_{: .bullet} The *language of rights* is incredibly *powerful* for *rallying people against* [injustice]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#justice-and-injustice). When a perceived right is violated, it *evokes strong emotional responses* and can mobilise collective action to demand redress or institutional change. This is the [Myth vs. Myth]({{ "/case-studies/us-vs-them/" | relative_url }}) dynamic at play, where *a shared narrative drives social movements*.

- _The "Lock-In" of Rights:_{: .bullet} Once declared and embedded in laws and constitutions, rights acquire significant institutional [lock-in]({{ "/case-studies/locked-in/" | relative_url }}). They are defended by courts, legal professions, and international organisations. Overturning a recognized right becomes incredibly difficult, even when *faced with new* [evidence]({{ "/guide/first-principles/evidence/" | relative_url }}) *or changing social priorities*. This can be beneficial (*protecting* [minorities]({{ "/case-studies/us-vs-them/majorities-vs-minorities/" | relative_url }})) or problematic (e.g., a "right" that *prevents* necessary collective action, or the "right" to unlimited pollution).

- _The Mismatch of Unlimited Claims vs. Limited Resources:_{: .bullet} A core challenge with rights, particularly "positive rights" (e.g., to *healthcare, housing, education*), is that they imply a corresponding obligation on the part of the state or [society]({{ "/case-studies/society/social-structures/" | relative_url }}) to *provide resources*. However, *resources are always* _finite_{: .warn}. This creates a "Mismatch" where the ideal of universal provision clashes with the reality of scarcity, leading to difficult trade-offs and political conflict over *whose "rights" take precedence*.

- _Rights as a Product of Power, Not Prior to It:_{: .bullet} While the narrative claims rights exist prior to power, in reality, the enforcement of rights is *an exercise of power*. Without police, courts, and societal [consensus]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#consensus) to enforce them, rights are *mere words on paper*. Those who hold power ultimately *decide which rights are protected, for whom, and to what extent*. 

_The enforcement of rights is an exercise of power._{: .mark}

### Step 4: Reflect on Human History – The Mismatch of Universalism and Evolved Partiality

The modern concept of universal, inherent rights represents a profound "mismatch" with much of our evolutionary history and core human [psychology]({{ "/guide/human-history/psychology/" | relative_url }}).

#### The Mismatch of Universalism vs. Tribalism

Our [evolved psychology]({{ "/guide/human-history/evolution/" | relative_url }}) is inherently **tribal**, prioritizing the well-being and rights of our in-group (kin, close community) over those of out-groups. The idea that all 8 billion humans possess identical, universal rights is a highly abstract, cognitive achievement that constantly battles against our ancient, emotionally driven partiality. The gap between declared universal rights and their discriminatory application globally (e.g., treatment of refugees, minorities) is *a stark illustration of this mismatch*.

#### The Mismatch of Abstract Individualism vs. Social Interdependence

While rights often focus on the individual, *our species is fundamentally interdependent*. For most of our history, individual [survival]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#survival) and well-being were **inextricably linked to the group**. The Enlightenment's emphasis on individual, atomized rights can sometimes create a "mismatch" by downplaying *our inherent sociality* and the *reciprocal obligations* that bind communities together, leading to phenomena like extreme individualism or societal atomisation (as discussed in [Liberalism]({{ "/case-studies/politics/political-ideas/liberalism/" | relative_url }})).

#### The Mismatch of Fixed Entitlements vs. Adaptive Norms

In ancestral [groups]({{ "/case-studies/society/social-structures/the-clan/" | relative_url }}), "rights" (e.g., to share resources, to participate in decisions) were often fluid, context-dependent, and enforced through dynamic [social norms]({{ "/case-studies/society/sociology/norms/" | relative_url }}) and reputation. *They were adaptive, changing with circumstances*. Modern, codified rights, while providing stability, can become rigid "lock-ins", slow to adapt to new moral challenges or societal needs, leading to calls for **new "rights"** (e.g., environmental rights, digital rights) that *reflect evolving concerns*.

#### The Mismatch of Absence of Formal "Rights" in Nature

In nature, *there are no "rights" in the human legal sense*. There is only the capacity to defend oneself, form alliances, and exploit opportunities. The idea of an animal having a "right" to not be eaten, for example, is a *human projection of* [moral values]({{ "/case-studies/society/sociology/values/" | relative_url }}) *onto a* **non-moral natural world**.

_This highlights that rights are indeed human constructions._{: .subtle}

The concept of rights is a powerful, recent post [Agricultural Transition]({{ "/guide/human-history/agricultural-transition/" | relative_url }}) **invention**, designed to manage the complexities of large-scale, anonymous societies, pushing against our more ancient, tribal, and context-dependent programming.

## Conclusion: A Verdict on a Powerful Invention, Not a Natural Law

If we had to get it right, as if the freedom and dignity of individuals depended on it—because it does—what would our conclusion be?

"Rights" are not inherent or objectively discoverable in nature. They are brilliant and indispensable intersubjective inventions—social technologies designed *to solve fundamental problems of power, cooperation, and human vulnerability in complex societies*. Their effectiveness and reality depend entirely on *collective belief*, *institutional enforcement*, and *a continuous societal commitment to their principles*.

- _Invented, Not Discovered:_{: .bullet} Rights are human constructs, born out of specific historical needs and philosophical debates, not plucked fully formed from a "state of nature".

- _Pragmatically Essential:_{: .bullet} Despite their invented nature, rights serve crucial pragmatic functions: *they constrain arbitrary power, foster predictability for cooperation, and provide a moral language for articulating and fighting against injustice*.

- _Fragile and Contested:_{: .bullet} Rights are _never truly "inalienable"_{: .warn} in practice. They exist only where they are actively defended, legislated, and enforced. They are constantly reinterpreted, challenged, and subject to the push and pull of political power and resource limitations.

A [scientific approach]({{ "/guide/first-principles/scientific-method/" | relative_url }}) to rights demands a pragmatic, evidence-based stance:

- _Focus on Enforcement, Not Just Declaration:_{: .bullet} Instead of debating their "natural" origin, we should focus on the objective mechanisms required for rights to be real: robust legal systems, independent judiciaries, civic education, and a culture of accountability.

- _Acknowledge Trade-offs and Resource Limits:_{: .bullet} Particularly for positive rights, we must openly discuss the trade-offs, opportunity costs, and resource implications. Universal rights are aspirational, and achieving them requires concrete, testable policy interventions.

- _Combat Selective Application:_{: .bullet} Systematically identify and challenge the "mismatches" where declared universal rights are _selectively applied_{: .warn} or denied based on tribal identity, social status, or political affiliation. *This means rigorous auditing of how rights are experienced by different groups*.

- _Evolutionary, Not Static:_{: .bullet} Recognise that the "list" of rights is not fixed but evolves with societal needs and technological change. *What constitutes "liberty" or "privacy" in the digital age requires ongoing, evidence-based re-evaluation*.

By understanding rights as powerful, yet invented, tools, we can move beyond *unproductive philosophical debates* about their origins and instead focus our energy on the practical, testable challenge of making them a more consistent and equitable reality for all.

_Invented to protect, sustained by power._{: .mark}
