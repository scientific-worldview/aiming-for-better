### Overview: The Dream of No Rulers

Anarchism, at its core, is a political philosophy that advocates for the abolition of all involuntary, [coercive]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#coercion) forms of hierarchy and [authority]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#authority), particularly the state. Its adherents envision societies organized through voluntary association, mutual aid, and self-governance, *believing that human beings are capable of creating order without the imposition of rulers*. The prevailing [narrative]({{ "/case-studies/narratives/" | relative_url }}) of anarchism is one of ultimate [freedom]({{ "/case-studies/law/pragmatic/liberty/" | relative_url }}), individual [autonomy]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#autonomy), and a rejection of the [corruption]({{ "/case-studies/politics/bureaucratic-machine/corruption/" | relative_url }}) and [oppression]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#oppression) inherent in all forms of state [power]({{ "/case-studies/politics/governance/power/" | relative_url }}). It is often presented as the purest expression of liberty, a system where individuals can truly flourish unhindered by external command.

![Anarchism]({{ "/assets/images/casestudies/politics/anarchy.webp" | relative_url }})

This case study will argue that, from a [scientific worldview]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#scientific-worldview), while anarchism's critiques of state power often *highlight real dysfunctions and* [lock-ins]({{ "/case-studies/locked-in/" | relative_url }}) (as seen in [The Bureaucratic Machine]({{ "/case-studies/politics/bureaucratic-machine/" | relative_url }})), its core premise represents a significant "[Mismatch]({{ "/case-studies/mismatches/" | relative_url }})" with the requirements for stable, large-scale [cooperation]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#cooperation) among non-kin humans. While small, high-trust [groups]({{ "/case-studies/society/social-structures/the-clan/" | relative_url }}) might spontaneously self-organise, the objective realities of managing public goods, externalises, and conflict resolution in complex, anonymous societies pose _insurmountable challenges_{: .warn} for a purely stateless model. We will examine how the *"dream of no rulers"* often overlooks fundamental aspects of [evolutionary psychology]({{ "/guide/human-history/psychology/" | relative_url }}) and *the pragmatic need for formalized mechanisms of collective action*, leading to either chaos, the emergence of informal power structures, or a return to coercion.

### Historical Perspective/Background Information

Anarchist ideas have roots in various philosophical traditions, though it emerged as a distinct political movement in the 19th century:

#### Ancient Roots

Elements of anarchist thought can be found in ancient philosophical schools (e.g., Taoism, Cynicism) and some early Christian movements, which *questioned worldly authority and advocated for simple, community-based living*.

#### Enlightenment Influences

Thinkers like William Godwin (late 18th century) are considered forerunners, arguing against the state *based on a* [belief]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#beliefs) *in human perfectibility and reason*.

#### 19th Century Emergence

Modern anarchism coalesced in the wake of the Industrial Revolution and the rise of powerful nation-states. Key figures included **Pierre-Joseph Proudhon** (who famously declared "*Property is theft*"), **Mikhail Bakunin** (a prominent critic of both [capitalism]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#capitalism) and Marxism), and **Peter Kropotkin** (who emphasized mutual aid as an [evolutionary]({{ "/guide/human-history/" | relative_url }}) factor). *These thinkers often critiqued both state power and private capitalism*.

#### Diverse Schools

Anarchism is not a monolithic ideology. It includes:

- _Anarcho-communism:_{: .bullet} Advocating for stateless, classless societies based on *common ownership of the means of production*.

- _Anarcho-syndicalism:_{: .bullet} Focusing on revolutionary labour unions to create a stateless society.

- _Individualist Anarchism:_{: .bullet} Emphasizing individual liberty above all else, sometimes leading to market-based solutions (anarcho-capitalism).

#### Historical Attempts

Various attempts to establish anarchist or semi-anarchist societies have occurred, such as during the Spanish Civil War (Catalonia), the Paris Commune, and some utopian communities. These were typically short-lived or violently [suppressed]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#suppression), *often facing the challenge of external state power*.

This history reveals anarchism as a persistent challenge to established authority, *driven by an ideal of absolute freedom*.

### Step 1: Wipe the Slate Clean

Let’s set aside the revolutionary imagery and the philosophical treatises. We start with a [clean slate]({{ "/guide/clean-slate/" | relative_url }}) to understand the core claims of anarchism.

#### The Problem

All forms of centralized authority, especially the state, are *inherently corrupting, coercive, and lead to oppression,* [inequality]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#inequality)*, and the suppression of individual liberty*.

#### The Stated Need

A system is needed that allows individuals and communities to self-organise, cooperate, and resolve disputes *without any involuntary* [hierarchy]({{ "/case-studies/mismatches/top-down-control/" | relative_url }}) *or coercive power structure*.

#### The Prevailing Narrative

Humans are fundamentally good, [rational]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#rational), and capable of voluntary cooperation. *The state is the primary impediment to this* [natural]({{ "/guide/human-history/natural-vs-normal/" | relative_url }}) *order*. Abolishing the state (and often private property) will unleash human potential and lead to _genuinely free and egalitarian_{: .cool} societies based on mutual aid. This [frames]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#framing) [society]({{ "/case-studies/society/social-structures/" | relative_url }}) as naturally ordered without external control.

_This narrative often suggests that coercion is unnatural and that, left to their own devices, humans will self-organise harmoniously._{: .subtle}

### Step 2: Applying First Principles

#### Testability

The claims of anarchism are, in principle, [testable]({{ "/guide/first-principles/testability/" | relative_url }}), though ethically and practically challenging on a large scale:

- _Small-Scale Experiments:_{: .bullet} We can objectively study existing or historical small-scale communities that attempt to operate without formal hierarchy, measuring their stability, decision-making efficiency, and conflict resolution mechanisms.

- _Public Goods Provision:_{: .bullet} We can analyse how stateless communities or regions manage the provision of essential public goods (e.g., infrastructure, defence, sanitation) without centralized taxation or enforcement.

- _Externalities Management:_{: .bullet} We can observe how effectively anarchic systems address negative externalities (e.g., pollution, resource depletion) *without a coercive body* to regulate [behaviour]({{ "/guide/human-history/psychology/" | relative_url }}).

- _Conflict Resolution:_{: .bullet} We can measure the incidence and resolution of interpersonal and inter-group conflicts in stateless societies, assessing the prevalence of violence or informal power dynamics.

#### Objective vs. Intersubjective

- _Objective:_{: .bullet} The objective fact that humans in large, anonymous groups often face ["tragedy of the commons"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy_of_the_commons){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} problems, or that informal [hierarchies]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#hierarchies) tend to emerge even in purportedly leaderless groups, is observable. The objective difficulty of coordinating large-scale collective action without [explicit rules]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#rules) and enforcement is measurable.

- _Intersubjective:_{: .bullet} The deep-seated belief that [human nature]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#human-nature) is inherently good and cooperative in the absence of authority, or that individuals will always act rationally and voluntarily for the common good, is a _powerful intersubjective narrative_{: .bold}. This ideal provides a moral foundation for anarchist thought but must be tested against [empirical evidence]({{ "/guide/first-principles/evidence/" | relative_url }}) of human behaviour in diverse contexts.

#### Only EVIDENCE Matters

_We must move beyond the appealing ideal of absolute freedom._{: .mark}

We must look at the data.

- Does the absence of formal state authority reliably lead to more free, [egalitarian]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#egalitarian), and stable societies, especially on a large scale?

- Do humans, without coercive structures, consistently manage public goods, resolve conflicts, and prevent the emergence of informal power dynamics?

- What does our [evolved psychology]({{ "/guide/human-history/psychology/" | relative_url }}) tell us about the conditions necessary for large-scale cooperation among non-kin?

### Step 3: Identify the Objective Consequences – The Inevitable Problems of Statelessness

_The data reveals the predictable dysfunctions of statelessness._{: .mark}

When we objectively examine historical and theoretical attempts at anarchic societies, we often find that the absence of formal state authority leads to predictable and significant dysfunctions, rather than the intended harmonious order.

#### The Emergence of Informal Power

Power does not disappear in the absence of a formalized state; *it merely decentralises and often becomes less accountable*. Informal hierarchies (*based on charisma, physical strength, wealth, social connections, or control of information*) almost _inevitably emerge_{: .warn}. These uncodified power structures can be far less transparent, predictable, and responsive to collective needs than a formal, rule-bound state, creating new avenues for arbitrary influence.

#### The Public Goods Dilemma

Many essential societal functions are "public goods" (e.g., defence, large-scale infrastructure, environmental protection). Anarchism struggles profoundly with the _"free rider problem"_{: .bold} – *individuals benefiting from these goods without contributing*. Without a coercive mechanism (like taxation and enforcement) to compel contributions, providing these goods on a large, complex scale becomes extremely difficult, *leading to chronic underprovision and* _collective failure_{: .warn}.

#### Unmanaged Externalities

Negative externalities (e.g., pollution, resource depletion from over-[exploitation]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#exploitation)) are *actions by one party that harm others without those others being compensated*. A stateless society lacks a central authority to internalise these costs through [regulation]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#regulation) or taxation, often resulting in _environmental degradation_{: .warn} and _unsustainable resource use_{: .warn} driven by individual self-interest.

#### Ineffective Dispute Resolution

In any human society, *conflicts of interest and disagreements will arise*. Without a universally recognized, impartial, and coercive arbiter (courts, police) whose judgments are widely accepted and enforced, disputes can easily escalate into cycles of vengeance or resort to _"might makes right"_{: .bold}. Private "protection agencies" or local militias, sometimes proposed in anarcho-capitalist models, can quickly devolve into competing, self-interested coercive powers, leading to *instability rather than* [justice]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#justice-and-injustice).

#### Vulnerability to External Coercion

Historically, attempts at stateless societies have proven *highly vulnerable to external state powers or organized groups that* _do possess coercive authority_{: .bold}. The ideal of non-coercion offers little practical defence against those willing to use force, often *leading to* _subjugation_{: .warn} *by external actors rather than sustained independence*.

_The objective consequence of abolishing formal rule, therefore, is not necessarily sustained freedom or harmony, but often a vacuum filled by less accountable forms of power, severe collective action failures, and a persistent vulnerability to internal and external coercion._{: .subtle}

### Step 4: Reflect on Human History – The Great Mismatch

Anarchism's vision of stateless harmony represents a profound "mismatch" with fundamental aspects of our [evolved human nature]({{ "/guide/human-history/evolution/" | relative_url }}) and the challenges of large-scale cooperation.

#### The Mismatch of Tribal Scale vs. Civilizational Complexity

Our ancestors lived in small, high-trust, kin-based groups where informal social pressure, direct [reciprocity]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#reciprocity), and shared [norms]({{ "/case-studies/society/sociology/norms/" | relative_url }}) were *highly effective at maintaining order*. Anarchism attempts to scale these intimate, tribal dynamics to vast, anonymous, diverse populations. This is a _fundamental mismatch_{: .bold}. Without *the strong bonds of kinship and direct accountability*, large groups require formal rules, institutions, and enforcement to prevent free-riding and maintain cooperation.

#### The Mismatch of Human Nature (Egoism & Tribalism) vs. Idealized Cooperation

While humans *are capable of immense* [altruism]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#altruism) *and cooperation (*especially within in-groups*)*, we *also possess* evolved drives for [status]({{ "/case-studies/society/sociology/status/" | relative_url }}), self-interest, and the formation of [in-group/out-group]({{ "/case-studies/us-vs-them/" | relative_url }}) distinctions (as discussed in [Political Tribalism]({{ "/case-studies/politics/political-dynamics/tribalism/" | relative_url }})). Anarchism often _idealises_{: .bold} human nature, assuming that these drives will simply disappear or be harmoniously managed without any coercive framework. History and [evolutionary psychology]({{ "/guide/human-history/psychology/" | relative_url }}) suggest that, on _a large scale_{: .bold}, the absence of enforceable rules can *unleash destructive aspects of human nature*.

#### The Mismatch of Spontaneous Order vs. Deliberate Design

While "spontaneous order" can emerge in some complex systems, maintaining a stable, prosperous [civilisation]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#civilisations) requires _deliberate institutional design_{: .cool} for managing common resources, defending against threats, and resolving intractable disputes. Anarchism's rejection of such design in favour of pure spontaneity is a mismatch with the requirements for robust, large-scale societal functioning.

#### The Mismatch of Absolute Freedom vs. Necessary Constraint

True freedom in a complex society is not the absence of all constraints, but the presence of just constraints that protect the freedom of all. *Unrestrained individual freedom can quickly impinge on the freedom of others*. Anarchism's pursuit of absolute individual freedom often overlooks the pragmatic necessity of collective constraints and common rules to prevent a descent into a *"war of all against all"* (Hobbes), which from an evolutionary perspective, would be highly suboptimal for [survival]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#survival) and [flourishing]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#human-flourishing).

_The dream of no rulers, while appealing to an [innate]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#innate) desire for freedom, is a profound mismatch with the objective realities of human psychology and the practical requirements for stable, large-scale cooperation beyond the intimate, kin-based group._{: .subtle}

## Conclusion: A Verdict on a Beautiful but Flawed Ideal

If we had to get it right, as if the stability and liberty of our societies depended on it—because it does—what would our conclusion be?

Anarchism, as a critique of state overreach and a vision of ultimate individual liberty, provides *a valuable philosophical challenge to established power structures*. It *highlights the potential* for [corruption]({{ "/case-studies/politics/bureaucratic-machine/corruption/" | relative_url }}), coercion, and inefficiency within [state institutions]({{ "/case-studies/politics/bureaucratic-machine/state-institutions/" | relative_url }}). However, its core premise of a stable, large-scale society functioning without any coercive authority represents a fundamental Mismatch with _evolutionary human nature_{: .bold} and *the objective challenges of collective action*:

- _The Vacuum of Power:_{: .bullet} Power is not abolished in a stateless society; it merely becomes decentralized, informal, and often less accountable. *This can lead to the emergence of new, potentially more oppressive, forms of control*.

- _The Public Goods Dilemma:_{: .bullet} Without a mechanism for collective enforcement and resource allocation, the provision of essential public goods and the management of shared resources become critically problematic.

- _Human Nature's Constraints:_{: .bullet} While humans are cooperative, they are also prone to self-interest, tribalism, and conflict. The absence of formalized, legitimate mechanisms for conflict resolution and norm enforcement *can lead to instability and violence*.

A [scientific approach]({{ "/guide/first-principles/scientific-method/" | relative_url }}), grounded in the Mismatch [Hypothesis]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#hypothesis) and evolutionary psychology, recognises that:

- _States are a Solution to Scale:_{: .bullet} The state, *despite its flaws*, is an evolved social technology—a necessary (though imperfect) solution to the problem of coordinating cooperation, managing public goods, and resolving conflict *among large groups of non-kin individuals*.

- _Formalized Institutions are Pragmatic:_{: .bullet} The institutions of governance ([laws]({{ "/case-studies/law/theory/" | relative_url }}), courts, enforcement) are not merely instruments of oppression; they are pragmatic tools for creating predictability, stability, and collective action, *without which individual freedom often diminishes*.

- _The Goal is Better Governance, Not No Governance:_{: .bullet} The challenge is not to abolish governance, but *to constantly reform and improve it*, making it *more accountable, less coercive, more efficient, and more aligned with human* [well-being]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#wellbeing), as explored in the [Foundations of Governance]({{ "/case-studies/politics/governance/" | relative_url }}) series.

> Ultimately, the _dream of no rulers_{: .bold}, *while beautiful in its idealism, is* _objectively unrealistic_{: .warn} *for complex, large-scale human societies*. 

A rational society must grapple with _the necessity of governance_{: .bold}, focusing its efforts on designing systems that _minimise coercion_{: .cool} while _maximizing cooperation_{: .cool}, *liberty, and the provision of collective goods*.

_True freedom requires functional governance to protect us from unaccountable power._{: .mark}
