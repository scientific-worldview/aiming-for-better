### Overview: The Wisdom of the Ancestors vs. Evidence

Conservatism, as a political philosophy, is often characterized by its emphasis on [tradition]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#tradition), established institutions, order, gradual change, and a skepticism towards radical reform or utopian visions. At its core, it frequently appeals to the _"accumulated wisdom"_{: .bold} of past generations, the stability of hierarchy, and the organic development of society. The prevailing [narrative]({{ "/case-studies/narratives/" | relative_url }}) of conservatism suggests that *"tried-and-true" methods, inherited customs, and institutions that have "withstood the test of time"* are inherently superior and safer than untested, rationalistic experiments in social engineering. It values stability, continuity, and *an acknowledgment of human imperfection*.

![Conservatism]({{ "/assets/images/casestudies/politics/tory.webp" | relative_url }})

This case study will argue that, from a [scientific worldview]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#scientific-worldview), while conservatism's emphasis on tradition and caution can, at times, align with *an* [evolved]({{ "/guide/human-history/evolution/" | relative_url }}) *human preference for stability and an aversion to risk*, it also presents significant "[Mismatches]({{ "/case-studies/mismatches/" | relative_url }})" with the dynamic, [evidence-driven]({{ "/guide/first-principles/evidence/" | relative_url }}) needs of a **rapidly changing world**. Its inherent skepticism towards change and its *reliance on inherited narratives* can lead to harmful [Lock-Ins]({{ "/case-studies/locked-in/" | relative_url }}) of _outdated ideas_{: .warn}, **unjust** [hierarchies]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#hierarchies), and **inefficient** systems, actively *resisting adaptation even in the face of overwhelming objective evidence*. We will explore when and why an appeal to tradition can be a pragmatic [heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}, and when it becomes an _ideological barrier_{: .warn} *to progress*, particularly when confronted with scientific discovery and new social realities.

### Background Information

While conservative impulses (e.g., maintaining social order, respecting elders) are likely as old as human society, conservatism as a *distinct political philosophy* largely emerged as *a reaction to the radical changes of the late 18th and early 19th centuries*:

- _The French Revolution (1789):_{: .bullet} Edmund Burke's 'Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790)' is often cited as *the foundational text of modern conservatism*. Burke vehemently criticized the abstract, rationalistic, and violent overthrow of established institutions, arguing instead for organic change, the wisdom of inherited prejudices, and the importance of social continuity. He saw [society]({{ "/case-studies/society/social-structures/" | relative_url }}) as a complex, delicate organism that should not be dismantled based on abstract theories.

- _Reaction to Enlightenment Rationalism:_{: .bullet} Conservatism developed in opposition to the Enlightenment's emphasis on universal reason, [individual rights]({{ "/case-studies/politics/governance/rights/" | relative_url }}), and the [belief]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#beliefs) that society could be rationally designed from [first principles]({{ "/guide/first-principles/" | relative_url }}). Conservatives typically argued that human reason is limited, and that complex societies evolve through countless generations, accumulating wisdom that no single generation can replicate or fully understand.

- _The 19th and 20th Centuries:_{: .bullet} Throughout this period, conservative thought adapted to various challenges: the rise of industrial [capitalism]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#capitalism), socialism, and mass [democracy]({{ "/case-studies/politics/democracy/" | relative_url }}). Different strands of conservatism emerged, sometimes *emphasizing free markets* (economic conservatism), sometimes *social hierarchy* (traditional conservatism), sometimes *national identity* (national conservatism).

- _Modern Conservatism:_{: .bullet} Contemporary conservatism often champions *free markets, limited government* (though not as strictly as Libertarianism), *strong national defence, and* ["traditional" social values]({{ "/case-studies/society/sociology/values/" | relative_url }}), sometimes appealing to [religious authority]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#authority) as a source of order and morality.

_This history shows conservatism as a philosophy deeply concerned with managing change, often by resisting it, and always by appealing to some form of inherited wisdom or order._{: .subtle}

### Step 1: Wipe the Slate Clean

Let’s set aside the political rhetoric and historical associations. We start with a [clean slate]({{ "/guide/clean-slate/" | relative_url }}) to understand the core claims of conservatism.

#### The Problem

Societies are complex, dynamic, and prone to disorder. Humans are *imperfect, prone to error*, and their attempts at radical social engineering can lead to chaos.

#### The Stated Need

A mechanism is needed to maintain stability, preserve valuable institutions, and guide change gradually, respecting the accumulated wisdom of the past.

#### The Prevailing Narrative

The best solution lies in respecting tradition, existing hierarchies, proven institutions, and an organic, gradual approach to change. It is skeptical of abstract theories and grand plans, favouring practical experience and the "test of time". 

_This [frames]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#framing) society as something that should evolve slowly, like a garden, rather than being built from scratch, like a machine._{: .subtle}

This narrative often suggests that what has survived, has survived for a reason, *implying a form of* [natural selection]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#natural-selection) *for social institutions*.

---

### Step 2: Applying First Principles

#### Testability

The claims of conservatism are, in many aspects, [testable]({{ "/guide/first-principles/testability/" | relative_url }}):

- _Institutional Resilience:_{: .bullet} We can objectively study whether societies that prioritise tradition and gradual change are more stable or less prone to violent upheaval compared to those undergoing rapid, radical reforms.

- _Policy Effectiveness:_{: .bullet} We can measure the outcomes of conservative policies (e.g., in economics, social welfare, [criminal justice]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#justice-and-injustice)) against stated goals and *compare them to alternative approaches*.

- _Wisdom of Crowds vs. Elites:_{: .bullet} We can test whether inherited customs or expert [consensus]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#consensus) (often a conservative appeal) are more effective [heuristics]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#heuristics) than rationalistic planning in complex social situations.

- _Adaptation to Change:_{: .bullet} We can observe whether societies clinging rigidly to tradition *are better or worse equipped to adapt to new technological, environmental, or social realities.*

#### Objective vs. Intersubjective

- _Objective:_{: .bullet} The objective fact that a particular institution (e.g., common law, a parliament) has endured for centuries is measurable. The _objective economic growth_{: .bold} or social stability of a nation following conservative policies is quantifiable.

- _Intersubjective:_{: .bullet} The belief that *"our way of life"* or *"our traditions"* are inherently superior, or that change is always dangerous, are powerful [intersubjective]({{ "/guide/first-principles/intersubjective/" | relative_url }}) narratives. The veneration of specific historical figures or foundational myths are **cultural constructs** that provide social cohesion but must be distinguished from objective evidence of their utility.

#### Only EVIDENCE Matters

We must move beyond the *emotional appeal* of "tradition" and the [fear]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#fear) of the unknown. _We must look at the data._{: .mark}

- When does tradition genuinely offer superior wisdom, and when does it become an obstacle to necessary adaptation?

- Do established institutions, *simply by virtue of their age*, always serve the *best interests of the current population*?

- What are the **measurable costs and benefits** of *resisting change* versus embracing it, in different contexts?

### Step 3: Identify the Objective Consequences – The Lock-Ins of Tradition

When we objectively examine the consequences of conservatism, we see that while *its cautious stance can be beneficial*, its adherence to tradition often creates powerful **"Lock-Ins"** that prevent necessary societal adaptation and *perpetuate inefficiencies or injustices*.

- _The "Status Quo Bias" Lock-In:_{: .bullet} Conservatism's *inherent preference for existing arrangements* creates a strong ["status quo bias"]({{ "/guide/thinking-for-yourself/cognitive-biases/" | relative_url }}). This is a psychological lock-in where the current state of affairs is implicitly preferred *simply because it is familiar*, even if there is objective evidence that alternatives would be better. *This leads to* [inertia]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#inertia) *in policy-making and a resistance to addressing* _systemic problems_{: .warn}.

- _The "Inherited Wisdom" Lock-In (Ignoring New Evidence):_{: .bullet} While genuine "ancestral wisdom" (e.g., about group [cooperation]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#cooperation), basic fairness) is real, conservatism often extends this to specific, culturally contingent traditions and institutions. This creates a "narrative lock-in" *where inherited practices are defended as wise simply because they are old*, even when new scientific, social, or economic evidence demonstrably proves them inefficient, unjust, or harmful (e.g., *outdated economic policies, social discrimination, resistance to climate action*).

- _The "Hierarchy and Order" Lock-In:_{: .bullet} Conservatism often values existing social hierarchies as *a source of order and stability*. But is it order and stability or the *vested interests* where those benefiting from the existing order (elites, established institutions) use conservative arguments to resist reforms that would challenge their [power]({{ "/case-studies/politics/governance/power/" | relative_url }}) or privilege, *even if those reforms would lead to greater overall* [well-being]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#wellbeing)?

- _The "Fear of the Unknown" Lock-In:_{: .bullet} Conservatism's skepticism towards rapid change taps into *an evolved human aversion to* [uncertainty]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#certainty) *and risk*. While this can be a valuable heuristic for avoiding reckless experiments, it can also become a "psychological lock-in" that paralyses societies, preventing them from making necessary adaptations in the face of novel challenges.

_The fear of potential negative consequences of change outweighs the objective evidence of current or impending harm from inaction._{: .subtle}

- _The "Organic Growth" Lock-In (Rejecting Deliberate Design):_{: .bullet} The conservative ideal of society evolving organically, like a garden, often leads to a rejection of deliberate, evidence-based social design. This can be a significant lock-in, as it implicitly denies the possibility of _rational improvement_{: .cool} and *maintains inefficient or suboptimal systems under the guise of "natural order"*.

The net effect can be a society that resists progress, maintains outdated practices, and finds itself increasingly out of sync with the objective demands of a dynamic world, *often to the detriment of its citizens*.

### Step 4: Reflect on Human History – The Mismatch of Stability and Adaptation

Conservatism, at cross purposes with reality, reflects a profound "mismatch" between our evolved need for social stability and the undeniable reality of _constant environmental and social change_{: .bold}.

- _The Mismatch of Tribal Stability vs. Civilizational Dynamism:_{: .bullet} Our [evolutionary history]({{ "/guide/human-history/" | relative_url }}) instilled in us *a strong preference for stability* and predictable social structures within our *small, tribal* [groups]({{ "/case-studies/society/social-structures/the-clan/" | relative_url }}). Rapid, chaotic change was often dangerous. Conservatism taps into this deep-seated need, providing comfort and continuity in a complex world. However, the [Transition]({{ "/guide/human-history/agricultural-transition/" | relative_url }}) to [civilisation]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#civilisations) introduced unprecedented *social and technological dynamism*. _Modern societies, by their very nature, are constantly evolving._{: .mark} A rigid adherence to *"stability at all costs"* becomes a **mismatch** with the *adaptive requirements of large, complex, and interconnected human systems*.

- _The Mismatch of Inherited Heuristics vs. Scientific Method:_{: .bullet} In ancestral environments, inherited rules-of-thumb and traditions (e.g., "don't eat that berry", "respect the elder") were vital, low-cost heuristics for [survival]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#survival) in *a relatively stable environment*. They represented practical, aggregated wisdom. Conservatism often *elevates these traditional heuristics* to *immutable principles*. This creates a mismatch with the [scientific method]({{ "/guide/first-principles/scientific-method/" | relative_url }}), which demands constant testing, revision, and *a willingness to abandon old ideas in the face of new evidence*. The "wisdom of the ancestors" is a powerful guide, but not a substitute for ongoing [empirical]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#empirical) inquiry.

- _The Mismatch of Local Wisdom vs. Universal Problems:_{: .bullet} Much of traditional wisdom is local and context-specific. What worked for a particular community in a particular time may not apply universally or across different eras. Conservatism's appeal to this localized, inherited wisdom can become a mismatch when societies face universal, interconnected problems (e.g., global pandemics, economic interdependence, human rights abuses) *that demand universal principles or globally coordinated responses*, rather than simply reiterating local customs.

- _The Mismatch of Hierarchy vs. Meritocracy/Adaptation:_{: .bullet} Many conservative traditions assume and defend existing social hierarchies. While some forms of hierarchy might emerge naturally in human groups (e.g., based on competence or age), rigid, inherited hierarchies (e.g., based on birth, fixed social class) can become a profound mismatch in a complex society that requires *adaptability* and _merit-based innovation_{: .cool}. Such systems can *prevent talented individuals from contributing, leading to a loss of valuable information and an inability to adapt to new challenges, ultimately undermining the very stability they seek to preserve*.

While a conservative impulse towards caution and valuing continuity is an evolved and often useful heuristic, a rigid, unscientific adherence to *"the way things have always been done"* is a dangerous mismatch with the dynamic, evidence-driven needs of modern life.

## Conclusion: A Verdict on Prudent Caution vs. Ideological Inertia

If we had to get it right, as if the adaptability and long-term well-being of our societies depended on it—because it does—what would our conclusion be?

Conservatism, in its ideal form, offers a valuable counter-balance to reckless reform, *reminding us of the complexity of society and the limits of human reason*. Its emphasis on caution, stability, and the value of existing institutions can align with *an evolved human aversion to unnecessary risk*. However, when applied rigidly and dogmatically, it becomes **a powerful Lock-In**, *perpetuating outdated systems* and actively resisting necessary adaptation in the face of overwhelming objective evidence.

- _The Double-Edged Sword of Tradition:_{: .bullet} Tradition can be a storehouse of valuable heuristics accumulated over generations. *But it can also be a prison of outdated ideas, preventing societies from adapting to new information and changing circumstances*.

- _Caution vs. Paralysis:_{: .bullet} A healthy skepticism towards radical change is prudent. But an [ideological]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#ideology) resistance to any change, or a defence of existing structures simply because they are old, *leads to* _societal paralysis_{: .warn} *and allows problems to fester and worsen*.

- _The Mismatch with Dynamic Reality:_{: .bullet} The world is not static. Technological, environmental, and social conditions are constantly evolving. A philosophy that prioritises inherited solutions over evidence-based adaptation is fundamentally mismatched with the requirements of a dynamic, complex civilisation.

A [scientific approach]({{ "/guide/first-principles/scientific-method/" | relative_url }}) to conservatism demands a nuanced and _adaptive perspective_{: .cool}:

- _Evidence-Based Respect for Institutions:_{: .bullet} Institutions and traditions should be respected not simply because they are old, but because there is objective evidence of their functional utility and positive contributions to well-being. *When the evidence suggests they are* [dysfunctional]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#dysfunction) *or unjust*, they must be *reformed or replaced*.

- _Pragmatic Gradualism:_{: .bullet} Change should ideally be gradual and iterative, allowing for constant testing and feedback, rather than revolutionary upheaval. *This is a scientific principle of experimentation*. However, "gradual" should not mean "never".

- _Distinguish Inherited Heuristics from Ideological Dogma:_{: .bullet} Recognise that some ancestral "wisdom" (e.g., cooperation, basic fairness, community bonds) are foundational. But distinguish these from culturally specific customs or institutions that are not universally applicable or empirically justified.

- _Embrace Adaptive Change:_{: .bullet} A truly "conservative" approach, in a scientific sense, would paradoxically be *highly adaptive*. It would seek to conserve the functional well-being and stability of society *by constantly evaluating its systems* and courageously *adapting them based on the best available evidence*, rather than letting them ossify.

> Ultimately, a scientific worldview offers *a framework for distinguishing between prudent caution*, which allows for robust, _evidence-based adaptation_{: .cool}, and _ideological inertia_{: .bold}, *which blindly clings to the past, often to the detriment of the present and future*.

_True stability requires evidence-based adaptation, not blind adherence to tradition._{: .mark}
