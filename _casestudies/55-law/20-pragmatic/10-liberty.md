### Overview

In any society, [laws]({{ "/case-studies/law/theory/" | relative_url }}) are _tools_{: .bold}. But the question is: *how many do we really need?*

Should laws _tightly regulate_{: .bold} our lives to protect us from every risk — even from ourselves?  
Or should they step back and protect only against _direct harm_{: .cool}, rather than **indirect harm** such as the "harm" caused by offence, discomfort, or [societal reaction]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#societal-reactions)?

![Liberty]({{ "/assets/images/casestudies/law/liberty.webp" | relative_url }})

This case study does not assume an answer to the question of [Liberty]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#liberty). Instead, it approaches the question the way this project always does: by starting with a [clean slate]({{ "/guide/clean-slate/" | relative_url }}), applying [core principles]({{ "/guide/first-principles/" | relative_url }}) like [testability]({{ "/guide/first-principles/testability/" | relative_url }}) and [evidence]({{ "/guide/first-principles/evidence/" | relative_url }}), and drawing on [human history]({{ "/guide/human-history/" | relative_url }}) — not _ideology_{: .neon}, [fear]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#fear), or moral instinct.

---

### Historical Perspective / Background Information

For most of human history, societies functioned with *very few formal laws*. Long before written codes, people relied on shared norms, reputation, [reciprocity]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#reciprocity), and local [consensus]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#consensus) to regulate [behaviour]({{ "/guide/human-history/psychology/" | relative_url }}) — what we now recognise as [social norms]({{ "/case-studies/society/sociology/norms/" | relative_url }}).

As populations grew and societies became more hierarchical — particularly after agriculture, property ownership, and centralized [authority]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#authority) emerged — written laws expanded rapidly. Law increasingly shifted from resolving disputes to *controlling behaviour*, often to protect elites, property, or political stability.

Modern nation-states inherited this structure and expanded it dramatically. Today, many societies operate under tens of thousands of criminal offences and regulations — — a scale that would have been *unimaginable* for most of *human existence.*

This historical backdrop matters because it reminds us that _heavy legal regulation_{: .warn} is not the default state of human society, but **a relatively recent development**.

### Step 1: Wipe the Slate Clean

Let’s set aside personal feelings and [intersubjective]({{ "/guide/first-principles/intersubjective/" | relative_url }}) [beliefs]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#beliefs) like:

- “Too many laws mean *tyranny*.”
- “The government should *protect people* from themselves.”
- “Freedom means *doing whatever* you want.”
- “We need law and order for [society]({{ "/case-studies/society/social-structures/" | relative_url }}) to *function*.”

These ideas are emotionally compelling — but they are not testable on their own. They are beliefs, not evidence.

Instead, we ask a simpler question:

_What can we observe, measure, and evaluate about how laws actually function in the real world?_{: .mark}

---

### Step 2: What’s the Point of Law?

Before debating quantity, we need clarity on purpose.

A legal system — in any form — should aim to:

- Prevent or reduce *unjust harm*
- Promote *social stability* and fairness
- Allow *peaceful resolution* of disputes
- Protect [individual rights]({{ "/case-studies/politics/governance/rights/" | relative_url }}) and property
- Create *predictability* so people can plan and cooperate
- Enable *accountability*, especially for those with [power]({{ "/case-studies/politics/governance/power/" | relative_url }})

These are real-world outcomes. They can be compared, measured, and evaluated.

So the question becomes:

_Do more laws achieve these outcomes — or do fewer, clearer laws do it better?_{: .mark}

---

### Step 3: Objective Consequences — What Can We Measure?

To evaluate how many laws we *should* have, we can ask:

- Does legal expansion lead to *less harm* or just *more punishment*?
- Does *complexity* improve compliance — or reduce it?
- Do simpler legal systems correlate with [greater wellbeing]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#wellbeing)?
- Are some systems more *adaptable* or *less corrupt* than others?
- Do criminal laws actually prevent harm — or merely respond after the fact?
- Do civil systems enable [cooperation]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#cooperation) more effectively than criminal sanctions?

These are [empirical]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#empirical) questions. Different societies provide real-world test cases.

---

### Step 4: Human History — From Informal Norms to Complex Codes

Before police forces, courts, and written law, hunter-gatherer societies relied on [social norms]({{ "/case-studies/society/sociology/norms/" | relative_url }}), reputation, and collective enforcement.

- Codified laws were *minimal or nonexistent*.
- Serious wrongdoing was handled communally.
- Responses were often restorative rather than punitive.
- Leadership was fluid; authority was negotiated, not imposed.

_The key insight:_{: .bold} law existed, but it was *local, minimal, and adaptive*.

Formal legal systems expanded with agriculture, property, [hierarchy]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#hierarchies), and large populations. Written codes like Hammurabi’s or Roman law emerged alongside centralized power — often serving control as much as [justice]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#justice-and-injustice).

In the modern era, many democracies have become *law-dense*, with expanding criminal codes and regulation that is not always proportional to the harm addressed.

---

### Step 5: Evidence From the Modern World

Different societies offer contrasting approaches.

#### United States

- Extremely _complex legal framework_{: .bold} at federal and state levels.
- Outcomes include:
  - The highest incarceration rate in the world
  - _Severe punishment_{: .warn} for minor offences
  - Heavy compliance burdens for citizens and businesses

#### France and Germany
- Detailed civil law systems with codified statutes.
- Often provide predictability in civil matters, but can create _bureaucratic rigidity_{: .warn}.

#### New Zealand
- Noted for relatively lean regulation, especially in business law.
- High rankings for transparency, low [corruption]({{ "/case-studies/politics/bureaucratic-machine/corruption/" | relative_url }}), and ease of participation.

#### Nordic Countries
- Fewer laws, _written plainly_{: .cool}.
- High compliance, strong trust, and pragmatic enforcement.

#### Singapore
- Efficient and minimalist in some areas.
- Also demonstrates that *fewer laws* alone do not guarantee [liberty]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#liberty), given strict enforcement and limited political freedoms.

**Key insight:**  
*Fewer laws do not automatically create freedom — but more laws often create complexity,* [inequality]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#inequality) *, and unintended harm.*

---

### Step 6: When Does Law Cause More Harm Than Good?

Law becomes counterproductive when it:

- Criminalises private, _consensual behaviour_{: .warn} (e.g. [sex]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#sexuality) work, drug use, or free expression)
- Uses _vague or elastic_{: .warn} categories (“antisocial”, “disturbing”, “suspicious”, "indecent")
- Is enforced _unevenly_{: .warn} across social groups (who gets arrested, who gets the best legal representation, etc)
- _Stifles_{: .warn} innovation or voluntary cooperation
- Produces _net harm_{: .warn} rather than [harm reduction]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#harm-reduction)

At that point, law shifts from preventing harm to *managing behaviour*. The question then becomes not just *how many* laws we have, but *what kind*. Are they rooted in **preventing actual harm** — _or controlling behaviour that simply offends societal norms_{: .mark}?

---

### Step 7: Can Civil Law Do More Than Criminal Law?

Some of the most _effective legal systems_{: .cool} do not punish — they *enable*.

- _Contract law_{: .bold} supports cooperation between strangers.
- _Corporate law_{: .bold} allows shared ownership and risk.
- _Tort law_{: .bold} provides redress without imprisonment.

These systems solve problems while leaving personal [freedom]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#autonomy) largely intact. They are _predictable, testable, and adaptable_{: .cool} — hallmarks of a [scientific approach]({{ "/guide/first-principles/scientific-method/" | relative_url }}) to governance.

---

### Step 8: Balancing Liberty With Protection

A free society doesn’t mean a lawless one. But it means being **very careful** about what we criminalise.

**The** [Scientific Worldview]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#scientific-worldview) **Suggests:**

- Laws should be _evidence-based_{: .cool}: grounded in data about actual, direct harm, not the "harm" caused by fear, [tradition]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#tradition) or societal reactions.
- Laws should be _minimized_{: .bold}: if a civil remedy or voluntary alternative works, it should be preferred.
- Laws should be _testable and revisable_{: .cool}: bad laws should be easier to repeal.
- Laws should *never exist for their own sake*: they’re a means, not a moral endpoint.

---

## Conclusion: Should We Minimise Laws?

If forced to answer plainly: _yes_{: .bold}.

A free society should aim for:

- Fewer criminal laws, tightly focused on real harm
- Strong civil systems that enable cooperation
- Simple, understandable rules
- Maximum personal liberty, with the burden of proof on restriction

_Excessive lawmaking usually reflects fear, not evidence._{: .subtle}

The best legal systems leave people alone — *not because* they don’t care, but because they *don’t need* to intervene.

_True liberty thrives under minimal, evidence-backed boundaries, never under dense legal control._{: .mark}
