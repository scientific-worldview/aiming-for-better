### Overview

Assisted dying is one of the most personal and emotionally charged topics in modern _public debate_{: .bold}. It touches *deep concerns* about pain, dignity, control, [compassion]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#altruism), and the role of [society]({{ "/case-studies/society/social-structures/" | relative_url }}) in matters of life and death.

Like abortion and drug [laws]({{ "/case-studies/law/theory/" | relative_url }}), this issue is often shaped by *powerful* [intersubjective]({{ "/guide/first-principles/intersubjective/" | relative_url }}) beliefs—[religious]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#religion), moral, and cultural. To cut through the noise, let's apply this website's _Thinking Guide_{: .cool}:

- Start with a [clean slate]({{ "/guide/clean-slate/" | relative_url }})

- Apply [first principles]({{ "/guide/first-principles/" | relative_url }}) (testability, objectivity, [evidence]({{ "/guide/first-principles/evidence/" | relative_url }}), reliability and the [scientific method]({{ "/guide/first-principles/scientific-method/" | relative_url }}))

- Consider [human history]({{ "/guide/human-history/" | relative_url }}) and evolutionary realities

- [Think for yourself]({{ "/guide/thinking-for-yourself/" | relative_url }})

---

### Setting Aside Untestable Beliefs

As with any public policy, we first set aside claims that *cannot be tested* or verified.

Arguments like “Assisted dying is a [sin]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#sin) or “Suffering has a divine purpose” are rooted in individual faith or [dogma]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#dogma). These may matter deeply to those who hold them, but *they can’t serve as the basis for laws **that affect everyone***.

_Instead, we focus on what can be observed, tested, and measured in the real world._{: .subtle}

---

### Identifying What We Can Measure

Rather than argue over abstract morals, we ask:

- Does assisted dying _reduce suffering_{: .cool} and *offer dignity*?

- Does it *risk* _coercing_{: .warn} vulnerable people into early death?

- Does it undermine *palliative care*, or work alongside it?

- Does legalising it lead to _“mission creep”_{: .warn}—more people becoming eligible over time?

- How do different _safeguards_{: .bold} affect outcomes?

_These are measurable outcomes with real-world data._{: .subtle}

---

### Looking at the Evidence

#### Oregon (USA) – The _Cautious Model_{: .neon}

The _Death with Dignity Act_{: .bold} (since 1997) is often seen as a model of [regulation]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#regulation). Its features:

- Only available to mentally competent adults with less than 6 months to live

- Requires confirmation by two doctors

- The request must be _voluntary_{: .cool}, and informed

Key findings after decades of data:

- No evidence of a _slippery slope_{: .warn}: Use has not expanded to _non-terminal patients_{: .bold}

- *Typical users are not vulnerable*: Most are educated, insured, and in _hospice care_{: .bold}

- _Motivation is autonomy:_{: .bullet} It's often *not about physical pain*, but *loss of dignity and control*

- Not all use the prescription: Around 1 in 3 never take the medication—**they just want the choice**

#### Canada – The _Expanding Model_{: .neon}

Canada introduced _Medical Assistance in Dying_{: .bold} (MAID) in 2016. Originally limited to the terminally ill, it has since expanded:

- Now includes people with _chronic conditions_{: .bold} or disabilities

- Plans to include _mental illness_{: .warn} as a sole qualifying condition are under review

Concerns raised:

- Slippery slope is real: Eligibility has *steadily widened*

- Some choose MAID due to [poverty]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#poverty) or _lack of care_{: .warn}, not just illness

- Raises questions about societal failure, not just individual choice

---

### Evaluating Risks and Counterarguments

Valid concerns include:

- _Social pressure:_{: .bullet} People may feel like a _burden_{: .warn} on family or society

- _Medical ethics:_{: .bold} Should doctors help people die? Does this change trust in care?

- _Failure to support life first:_{: .bullet} If people are choosing death due to poverty or _isolation_{: .warn}, are we failing them?

---

### Human History: What Did Our Ancestors Do?

Long before hospitals and pain relief, [early humans]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#early-humans) had to face the brutal reality of death in small, mobile [groups]({{ "/case-studies/society/social-structures/the-clan/" | relative_url }}). While we can’t point to ancient legal systems, we can infer from [anthropology]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#anthropology) and ethnography how these decisions were made.

#### Survival First

- In mobile bands, a person who was terminally ill, too weak to travel, or a danger to the group’s [survival]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#survival) posed a real dilemma

- The community had to choose: help them die, or leave them behind

#### Documented Practices

- _Voluntary abandonment:_{: .bullet} In some cultures, elderly people chose to _stay behind_{: .bold}, often with food and water, when they could no longer contribute

- _Assisted dying by request:_{: .bullet} In rare cases, family members helped end life when asked

- _Cultural acceptance:_{: .bullet} These actions weren’t seen as cruel but as pragmatic, necessary, and even honourable

This tells us: _the idea of ending life when it becomes unbearable or unsustainable is not new._{: .mark} It’s deeply human, and sometimes the most compassionate choice available.

---

### Weighing It All Up

- The Oregon-style model—with strict safeguards, terminal illness requirement, and voluntary, repeated requests—has held up well under scrutiny

- Canada's broader model has highlighted real risks, especially when social support is lacking

- What works depends not just on law, but on _robust systems of care_{: .cool}, equality, and monitoring

---

## Conclusion

If my life, family, freedom, or future depended on answering whether assisted dying should be legal for competent adults with terminal illness—
and if done with strong safeguards and oversight—
**I would answer yes**.

The best evidence shows it can reduce suffering, protect [autonomy]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#autonomy), and coexist with excellent _palliative care_{: .bold}—when done with *caution* and _compassion_{: .cool}.

Claims about the **“sanctity of life”** are not [testable]({{ "/guide/first-principles/testability/" | relative_url }}). But *suffering is real*. And history—ancient and modern—shows that choosing a dignified death when no good days remain is not unnatural. _It may be the most human act of all._{: .mark}

_Balancing personal autonomy with rigorous societal safeguards ensures a compassionate approach to end-of-life choices._{: .subtle}
