### Overview

Even with the *best intentions*, our thinking can be derailed by _built-in shortcuts and blind spots_{: .bold}.

Our [brains]({{ "/case-studies/who-we-are/brain/" | relative_url }}) [evolved]({{ "/guide/human-history/evolution/" | relative_url }}) for *quick decisions* in small social [groups]({{ "/case-studies/society/social-structures/the-clan/" | relative_url }}), not for _calmly weighing complex data in large societies_{: .mark}.
 
These _mental habits_{: .bold} can *save time* — but they can also *lead us astray*.

![Pitfalls]({{ "/assets/images/guide/thinking/bias.webp" | | relative_url }})

Learning to recognise these traps is a big step toward [thinking for yourself]({{ "/guide/thinking-for-yourself/" | relative_url }}).

---

### 1. Confirmation Bias

We tend to seek out, remember, and give more weight to information that _confirms what we already believe_{: .bold} — while _ignoring_{: .warn} or downplaying [evidence]({{ "/guide/first-principles/evidence/" | relative_url }}) that challenges it.

#### Example

If you believe a certain [diet]({{ "/case-studies/health/diet/" | relative_url }}) works, you may notice every success story but overlook failures or contrary studies.

- _Counter-move:_{: .bullet} Deliberately look for opposing evidence. Ask yourself: *"What would prove me wrong?"*

---

### 2. Overconfidence

Being more certain about our knowledge or predictions than the evidence justifies.

#### Example

Predicting election results with 100% confidence based on a single poll.

- _Counter-move:_{: .bullet} Attach probabilities to your claims and be ready to adjust them as new information comes in.

---

### 3. Motivated Reasoning

Shaping our _interpretation of evidence_{: .bold} to fit our desires, [values]({{ "/case-studies/society/sociology/values/" | relative_url }}), or identity — often *without realising it*.

#### Example

Dismissing valid criticism of your favourite political party because accepting it feels like a betrayal.

- _Counter-move:_{: .bullet} Separate *wanting* something to be true from **checking** whether it is.

---

### 4. Availability Bias

Judging the likelihood of events by how easily examples come to mind — not by actual data.

#### Example

After seeing several news reports about plane crashes, you overestimate the danger of flying compared to driving.

- _Counter-move:_{: .bullet} Look at statistics, not just vivid stories.

---

### 5. Anchoring

Letting the first piece of information you hear set the tone for your judgement — even if it’s irrelevant.

#### Example

If the first price you see for a car is £20,000, lower prices may feel like bargains even if they’re still high.

- _Counter-move:_{: .bullet} Research independently before forming expectations.

---

### 6. Hindsight Bias

After an event happens, believing you *“knew it all along”* — which can make you overconfident about predicting the future.

#### Example

Claiming you knew a company’s stock would rise after it already has.

- _Counter-move:_{: .bullet} Keep written records of your predictions to compare with actual outcomes.

---

### 7. Groupthink

Going along with the [consensus]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#consensus) to preserve harmony, rather than questioning whether the group’s decision is sound.

#### Example

Agreeing to a flawed plan in a meeting because everyone else seems enthusiastic.

- _Counter-move:_{: .bullet} Play devil’s advocate — or invite someone else to.

---

## Why This Matters

Recognising these _pitfalls_{: .warn} doesn’t make you immune to them — but it *gives you a fighting chance*.

[Thinking for yourself]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#thinking-for-yourself) means _noticing when your brain is taking shortcuts_{: .mark}, and *pausing long enough* to check whether those shortcuts are _leading somewhere useful_{: .bold}.
