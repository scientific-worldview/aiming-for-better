### Overview

**Fallacies** are errors in reasoning — arguments that might *sound* convincing but _break logical rules_{: .bold}.

They often crop up in debates, news articles, political speeches, and social media posts.

Spotting them is an *essential part of critical thinking*, because if the reasoning is _flawed_{: .warn}, _the conclusion is unreliable_{: .bold}.

![Fallacies]({{ "/assets/images/guide/thinking/fallacies.webp" | | relative_url }})

Fallacies come in many forms. Some are **deliberate attempts** to *persuade without evidence*; others are _honest mistakes_{: .bold}. Either way, learning to recognise them helps you _avoid being misled_{: .bold} — and stops you from accidentally _making the same mistakes yourself_{: .mark}.

---

### Why Fallacies Matter

A **flawed argument** can lead to *poor decisions*, *wasted resources*, or *harmful outcomes*. In public life, [fallacies]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#fallacies) can fuel [misinformation]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#misinformation), polarisation, and bad policy.  
In personal life, they can damage relationships or lead you to adopt [beliefs]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#beliefs) without _proper justification_{: .bold}.

_Think of them as mental "bugs" in reasoning._{: .subtle}

If your thinking process is like a computer program, fallacies are the coding _errors_{: .warn} that cause it to produce the wrong output.

### Common Types of Fallacies

Here are some of the most common — and most persuasive — fallacies you might encounter:

#### 1. Straw Man

Misrepresenting an opponent’s argument to make it _easier to attack_{: .bold}.

_"You think we should reduce military spending? So you want to leave the country defenceless?"_{: .subtle}

#### 2. Ad Hominem

Attacking the person _instead of addressing the argument_{: .bold}.

_"You can’t trust his views on climate change — he’s not even a scientist"._{: .subtle}

#### 3. Appeal to Authority

Assuming something is true simply _because an [authority]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#authority) figure says so_{: .bold}.  

_"This [diet]({{ "/case-studies/health/diet/" | relative_url }}) must work — a celebrity swears by it"._{: .subtle}

#### 4. Slippery Slope

Arguing that **one step** will *inevitably lead* to _a chain of negative events_{: .bold} *without* [evidence]({{ "/guide/first-principles/evidence/" | relative_url }}).

_"If we legalise this, next we’ll legalise everything!"_{: .subtle}

#### 5. False Dilemma (Either/Or)

Presenting only two options when more exist. 

_"You’re either with us or against us"._{: .subtle}

#### 6. Post Hoc (False Cause)

Assuming that because one event followed another, it was caused by it.

_"I wore my lucky socks and my team won — so the socks caused the win"._{: .subtle}

#### 7. Bandwagon (Appeal to Popularity)

Claiming something is true or good just _because many people believe it_{: .bold}.

_"Millions of people use this supplement, so it must work"._{: .subtle}

---

### How to Spot a Fallacy

- _Pause and examine_{: .bullet} the structure of the argument.  
- _Separate evidence from opinion_{: .bullet} — is the claim *supported by data* or just assertion?  
- _Ask what’s missing_{: .bullet} — are alternative explanations or possibilities ignored?  
- _Check for emotional manipulation_{: .bullet} — does the argument rely more on feelings than [facts]({{ "/guide/first-principles/correct-answers/" | relative_url }})?  

---

### Using This Knowledge

You don’t need to memorise every named fallacy. Instead, train yourself to:

- Notice when something doesn’t logically follow.
- Ask questions _before accepting a conclusion_{: .bold}.
- Use clear reasoning in your own arguments.
