### Overview: Facts?

Philosophers and scientists have long wrestled with deep questions about _what we can truly know_{: .mark}. One of the most famous challenges is known as [The Problem of Induction]({{ "/articles/philosophy/induction/" | relative_url }}): it points out that we can’t **logically prove** that the future will resemble the past. Just because the sun has risen every day so far *doesn’t guarantee* it will rise tomorrow.

_So how can we ever be sure of anything?_{: .subtle}

And yet, if your life or future depended on giving the right answer to the question “Will the sun rise tomorrow?”, your answer would be immediate and confident: “Yes, of course it will.”

![correct-answers]({{ "/assets/images/guide/first-principles/correct.webp" | relative_url }})

Your life would be saved—and rightly so. The sun will rise tomorrow. That’s the practical, [evidence-based]({{ "/guide/first-principles/evidence/" | relative_url }}), correct answer.

---

### Philosophy vs Common Sense

The _Problem of Induction_{: .bold} reminds us that *absolute [certainty]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#certainty) is hard to come by*. But demanding philosophical certainty in everyday life is not only impractical—it’s dangerous.

For example, microscopic germs can't be seen with the naked eye, and yes, in strict philosophical terms, germ [theory]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#theory) remains *"unfalsified"* rather than *"proven"*. But in practical terms—given **mountains of reliable evidence**—germs are a fact. No reasonable person insists on treating germ theory as *“just a theory”* while preparing food, going into surgery, or treating disease. To do so would be foolish.

We don’t need to hedge every answer with *“but we can’t really know for sure”*. At some point, we must be able to say:

_“This is a correct answer, based on overwhelming evidence.”_{: .subtle}

---

### Why This Matters

If we don’t accept the possibility of correct answers about [objective]({{ "/guide/first-principles/intersubjective/" | relative_url }}) reality, *we invite chaos*. When there are no right answers, there are also no wrong answers—and into that vacuum rush myths, [misinformation]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#misinformation), quackery, and even outright nonsense.

Historically, people didn’t always have the tools to investigate the world. They still asked questions—about disease, thunder, death, the stars—but without the means to [test their ideas]({{ "/guide/first-principles/testability/" | relative_url }}), they *made up stories*. These stories—origin myths, afterlife [beliefs]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#beliefs), supernatural explanations—were repeated, ritualized, and eventually became powerful [narratives]({{ "/case-studies/narratives/" | relative_url }}): widely shared, deeply held **beliefs** that were treated *as if they were facts*.

We still see this today. Whether [religious]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#religion) or secular, people continue to offer _sweeping explanations_{: .bold} for complex questions. Some are harmless. Others are not.

---

### A Practical Stance

We acknowledge that philosophical doubts exist, and if you're curious we invite you to read 👉 [The Problem of Induction]({{ "/articles/philosophy/induction/" | relative_url }}) But for all practical purposes, and for the sake of clarity, we take the following to be a [first principle]({{ "/guide/first-principles/" | relative_url }}) of this approach:

_There ARE correct answers to questions about the objective world._{: .subtle}

Those answers may be provisional or incomplete—but where reliable evidence converges overwhelmingly, we treat the answer as fact, unless and until new evidence proves otherwise.

---

## One Final Caution

This principle only applies to _objective phenomena_{: .bold}. As you’ll see soon, many people try to apply this kind of reasoning to intersubjective or subjective claims—areas where **“correct answers”** *don’t exist in the same sense*. That’s a critical distinction, and we’ll unpack it next.
