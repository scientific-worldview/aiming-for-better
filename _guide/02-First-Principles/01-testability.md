### Overview

Once we commit to [a scientific and evidence-based worldview]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#scientific-worldview), the first and most important filter we must apply is this:

> *If an idea cannot be tested, it must be **set aside***

In science, ideas are often called _hypotheses_{: .neon}. If a [hypothesis]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#hypothesis) cannot be tested through observation, [experiment]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#experiment), simulation, or any other [repeatable method]({{ "/guide/first-principles/scientific-method/" | relative_url }}), it has no place in serious inquiry—no matter how intriguing, comforting, or widely accepted it may be.

![Testability]({{ "/assets/images/guide/first-principles/mrTest.webp" | relative_url }})

This includes two broad types of untestable ideas:

---

### 1. Remote Possibilities

Philosophy, fiction, and [religion]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#religion) are filled with fascinating but untestable *"what if?"* scenarios—ideas that we’ll refer to here as "remote possibilities".

Take the film The Matrix, where the protagonist Neo discovers that his entire reality is a simulation. Or earlier still, consider the Greek philosopher Plato and his [Theory of Forms]({{ "/case-studies/philosophy/plato/" | relative_url }}), which posits that everything we see is merely a shadow of some perfect, non-physical reality. These ideas are *imaginative* and culturally influential, but they **cannot be tested**—and therefore cannot be used to make claims about reality.

Variations of this theme abound:

- "We might be a _brain in a vat_{: .bold}"

- "Reality could be an _illusion_{: .bold}"

- "A god might be manipulating the universe from beyond space and time"

No matter how *intriguing* these notions are, they lack any path to [empirical]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#empirical) testing. Without a way to observe or falsify them, they fall outside the boundaries of [rational]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#rational) inquiry.

This doesn’t mean we’re being “closed-minded”. In fact, rejecting untestable claims *protects our open-mindedness*—ensuring we don't get bogged down by endless, unresolvable speculation. If we allow one untestable idea into our [worldview]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#worldview), then logically we must allow *all of them*: [ghosts]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#agency-detection), hobgoblins, vampires & werewolves, alien soul harvesters, divine beings from every mythology, and so on. Since they are all *equally untestable*, they are all equally possible—but also mutually incompatible. The result? Paralysis, confusion, and intellectual chaos.

So we take the only consistent and intellectually honest position:

_We reject all remote possibilities as fictional or irrelevant until and unless they become testable._{: .subtle}

This includes religious claims such as the existence of God, Heaven, or other supernatural realms. These ideas may be meaningful to individuals or cultures—but they are not testable, and therefore *they cannot be part* of **an evidence-based worldview**.

---

### 2. Pseudoscience & "Sciency-Sounding" Ideas

The second category of untestable ideas includes concepts that sound scientific but *do not stand up to scientific scrutiny*. These often borrow the **language of science**—using terms like “energy,” “materialism,” or “analysis”—but remain untestable in practice.

Examples include:

- Freud’s "psychoanalytic structures" (id, ego, superego): compelling in [narrative]({{ "/case-studies/narratives/" | relative_url }}), but where is the testable evidence?

- Marx’s "dialectical materialism": an influential theory of history and society, but what empirical tests could confirm or falsify it?

- Postmodernism’s grand claims about language and [power]({{ "/case-studies/politics/governance/power/" | relative_url }}): *intellectually stylish*, but again, **untestable**.

These frameworks may have historical or *cultural value*, but unless they generate hypotheses that can be **put to the test**, they are not scientific.

For a young thinker, such ideas are captivating and appear to be the height of intellectual sophistication. Until you recognise that appeal, popularity, or philosophical elegance are not substitutes for [testability]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#testability). If we can detect gravitational waves—an incredibly difficult task—then surely we could detect the id or the ego if they actually existed as [objective]({{ "/guide/first-principles/intersubjective/" | relative_url }}) phenomena.

The failure to do so is not due to lack of tools or imagination, but because these constructs are likely not testable to begin with.

---

### Why This Principle Matters

_Testability_{: .bold} is the *first gatekeeper* in our approach to understanding the world. It’s how we separate **meaningful inquiry** from *endless speculation*.

This doesn’t mean we stop imagining, wondering, or creating stories. It means we *recognise the difference* between ideas worth testing and ideas we must **set aside** until they cross into the realm of [evidence]({{ "/guide/first-principles/evidence/" | relative_url }}).

Put simply:

_If it can’t be tested, it can’t be trusted_{: .mark}
