### The Problem of Induction

In the philosophy of science, the “**problem of induction**" is a philosophical challenge that highlights how we can't logically “prove” that the future will resemble the past. Just because the sun has risen every day so far, you can’t be 100% certain it will rise tomorrow. So philosophically, you cannot “deduce” universal [laws]({{ "/case-studies/law/theory/" | relative_url }}) in science; only “induce” them.

Karl Popper, in response to the problem of induction, argued that science doesn't progress by proving theories, but by attempting to disprove them. The hallmark of a [scientific theory/hypothesis is]({{ "/guide/first-principles/scientific-method/" | relative_url }}) its falsifiability - it must make predictions that can be potentially proven wrong by observation or [experiment]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#experiment).

As no [theory]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#theory) can ever be “proven” in the “deductive” sense, Popper argued that even our best scientific theories are always provisional and open to revision. They are only "tentatively accepted" as long as they stand up to repeated attempts at [falsification]({{ "/guide/first-principles/testability/" | relative_url }}). They are the "best we have so far" - not the final word.

However!

Are we really going to doubt that the sun will rise tomorrow? In everyday life, do we really raise such hocus-pocus uncertainties? Imagine an airline pilot announcing over the tannoy: “Sorry ladies and gents, while it can be proven, statistically (as mathematics is deductive as opposed to inductive), that aeroplanes are the safest form of travel, unfortunately, as science is not deductive, due to the philosophical “problem of induction” the airline cannot guarantee that the science of aerodynamics will hold for this flight. Despite millions of previous flights [evidencing]({{ "/guide/first-principles/evidence/" | relative_url }}) it is probably a [certainty]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#certainty), probably a “fact”, philosophically we cannot infer the future will resemble the past. Please enjoy your flight!”

The notion that all scientific knowledge is "tentative" flies in the face of common-sense. Are microscopic germs a tentative scientific model that explains how we get infected with some illness or disease or are they now fact? The rejection of certainty in [objective fact]({{ "/guide/first-principles/intersubjective/" | relative_url }}) leaves a kind of intellectual paralysis where one might wonder why bother learning about anything if it could all be wrong? And in practical terms, if everything is "tentative", how do we make decisions? Do we have to doubt everything constantly? This can seem impractical for normal human life.

### The Common-Sense Approach: Dawkins and Overwhelming Evidence

- _Beyond Falsification:_{: .bullet} While acknowledging Popper's contribution, some scientists like Richard Dawkins (and many others) argue that while theoretically all scientific knowledge is tentative, practically some theories are so well-supported by overwhelming evidence that for all intents and purposes, they are considered "[facts]({{ "/guide/first-principles/correct-answers/" | relative_url }})".
- _The Accumulation of Evidence:_{: .bullet} They point out that science doesn't rely on a single observation or experiment, but on the convergence of evidence from multiple independent sources.
- _Practical Certainty:_{: .bullet} When we see the successful application of aerodynamics in every flight that takes place, when we have the fossil record, genetic evidence, and observations of [evolution]({{ "/guide/human-history/evolution/" | relative_url }}) in action - they provide so much corroborating evidence that for most people, they should, practically speaking, be accepted as "fact".
- _Avoiding Paralysis:_{: .bullet} Dawkins and those who hold this view suggest that being overly fixated on theoretical uncertainty can lead to a kind of intellectual paralysis and a reluctance to act or believe, even when the evidence points strongly in one direction.
- _The Importance of Context:_{: .bullet} Importantly, this doesn't dismiss the Popperian ideal of continued scrutiny but suggests that this happens more at the level of research than in everyday understanding.

### Reconciling the Two Views

- _Levels of Certainty:_{: .bullet} The key is to recognise that there are different levels of certainty. Popper's view reminds us to always be open to the possibility of being wrong and that even the most well-supported theories are, in principle, revisable. However, Dawkins' view allows us to recognise that some theories are so well-established that we can treat them as practically certain, allowing us to build upon them and make sound decisions in daily life.
- _Scientific Humility vs. Practical Application:_{: .bullet} Science demands [humility]({{ "/guide/thinking-for-yourself/intellectual-humility/" | relative_url }}) and open-mindedness, always recognizing the possibility of error. However, it also allows us to make accurate predictions and build technologies that work reliably, which means we have good reason to trust them.
- _The Spectrum of Knowledge:_{: .bullet} Perhaps a good analogy is a spectrum of knowledge. At one end, you have completely unsupported ideas (e.g., conspiracy theories), in the middle, you have tentative theories with some supporting evidence, and at the other end, you have theories with overwhelming evidence, which allows a "common sense" approach to take precedence.

### In Summary

The arguments outlined highlights a critical discussion in understanding how scientific knowledge works.

- _Popper's emphasis on falsification_{: .bullet} is crucial for the scientific method - encouraging continuous self-correction and avoiding [dogmatism]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#dogma).
- _Dawkins' emphasis on "overwhelming evidence"_{: .bullet} provides a much-needed sense of certainty in our practical lives and encourages us to use science as our guide and to build and act with the knowledge available.

A balanced view involves accepting that scientific knowledge is always provisional in principle but that we can achieve practical certainty in many cases through an abundance of evidence. A critical, [rational]({{ "/glossary/" | relative_url }}#rational) thinker understands both sides, using the idea of "tentativeness" to avoid complacency, and at the same time, relying on the vast amount of evidence available to make sound decisions, acting with confidence but always with a level of humility.
